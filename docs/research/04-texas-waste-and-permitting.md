# Texas Permitting and Waste Rules for Sulfuric-Acid Processing of Tiros Titanium/Rare-Earth Material at Brownsville (Cameron County), Texas: with a Brief Brazil (Minas Gerais) Comparison

Research date: 4 November 2026. Dates are given for older sources. Abbreviations are spelled out on first use because the readers are not mining specialists.

Glossary of terms used below:
- TCEQ: Texas Commission on Environmental Quality, the state environmental regulator.
- DSHS: Texas Department of State Health Services, which runs the Texas Radiation Control Program.
- NRC: US Nuclear Regulatory Commission. An "Agreement State" (Texas is one) has taken over most NRC licensing inside its borders.
- NORM / TENORM: (Technologically Enhanced) Naturally Occurring Radioactive Material, meaning natural radioactivity that processing has concentrated.
- Source material: uranium and/or thorium at 0.05% by weight or more.
- 11e.(2) byproduct material: tailings or wastes from processing ore *primarily for its uranium or thorium content*, i.e. uranium mill tailings.
- RCRA: the federal Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, the hazardous-waste law. "Subtitle C" is its hazardous-waste program.
- Bevill exclusion: the RCRA exemption for most mining wastes and 20 named mineral-processing wastes.
- TPDES: Texas Pollutant Discharge Elimination System, the Texas wastewater discharge permit.
- NSR: New Source Review, the air-permit program for new or modified plants.
- SOAH: the State Office of Administrative Hearings, where contested permits are tried.
- MREC: mixed rare-earth carbonate, the intermediate rare-earth product.
- LLRW: low-level radioactive waste.
- WCS: Waste Control Specialists, a disposal company in Andrews County, Texas.

## Q1. Which Texas agencies regulate this plant, and how are radioactive materials (source material and NORM) licensed?

### Takeaway
A Brownsville acid plant would need several separate authorizations:
- TCEQ: air (NSR), wastewater (TPDES), storm water, and industrial solid waste registration and classification.
- A radioactive-materials authorization. Which agency issues it depends on how the feed, MREC, and residues classify. TCEQ has "sole and exclusive" authority over *source material recovery and processing* and over 11e.(2) byproduct disposal. DSHS regulates possession and use of NORM and most other radioactive materials. TCEQ (non-oilfield) and the Railroad Commission (oilfield) regulate NORM *disposal*.
- If any feed, MREC, or residue reaches 0.05% uranium plus thorium, it is source material, and the plant is very likely in TCEQ's radioactive-materials licensing program. That program is slow: roughly 1 to 2+ years based on TCEQ's own processing statistics.

### Cited Findings
**Agency split (statute)**
- Senate Bill 1604 (2007) moved most licensing for uranium recovery, byproduct material, and radioactive waste storage, processing, and disposal from DSHS to TCEQ. It gave TCEQ "sole and exclusive authority to grant, deny, renew, revoke, suspend, amend, or withdraw licenses for source material recovery and processing or for storage, processing, or disposal of by-product material." — [Texas Legislature, SB 1604 bill analysis](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/80R/analysis/html/SB01604F.htm)
- The current statute is Texas Health & Safety Code §401.2625: "The commission [TCEQ] has sole and exclusive authority to grant, deny, renew, revoke, suspend, amend, or withdraw licenses" for source material recovery and processing and for by-product material storage, processing, and disposal. — [Tex. Health & Safety Code §401.2625 (CounselStack)](https://law.counselstack.com/statute/tx/HS/401.2625)
- Health & Safety Code §401.011: DSHS "has jurisdiction over activities and substances regulated under this chapter except as provided by Subsection (b) and Subchapters E, F, G, and K." Under §401.011(b), TCEQ regulates waste disposal, certain waste processing and storage, source material recovery, and "sites for the disposal of ... naturally occurring radioactive material waste." — [Tex. Health & Safety Code ch. 401](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/HS/htm/HS.401.htm)
- The statutory definition of by-product material includes "tailings or wastes produced by or resulting from the extraction or concentration of uranium or thorium from ore processed primarily for its source material content" (§401.003(3)(B)). — [Tex. Health & Safety Code ch. 401](https://tcss.legis.texas.gov/resources/HS/htm/HS.401.htm)
- DSHS says NORM is split across three agencies:
  - DSHS regulates "receipt, possession, storage, use, and treatment" of NORM under 25 TAC §289.259.
  - The Railroad Commission regulates disposal of oil-and-gas NORM.
  - TCEQ regulates disposal of other NORM, and DSHS describes TCEQ's rules as "currently in the developmental stage."
  — [DSHS NORM Licensing page](https://dshs.texas.gov/texas-radiation-control/radioactive-material-licensing-radiation-control-program/norm-naturally-occurring-radioactive-material-licensing)

**Source material threshold**
- TCEQ defines source material as material containing "greater than 0.05 weight percent" uranium, thorium, or both. — [TCEQ NORM Waste Disposal page](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/permitting/radmat/uranium/norm.html)
- TCEQ guide RG-486 (July 2022):
  - Mixtures with less than 0.05% source material by weight are exempt under 25 TAC §289.251(d)(1).
  - Approximate activity equivalents: thorium-232 at 54.9 pCi/g (110 pCi/g total natural thorium); uranium-238 at 167.5 pCi/g (340 pCi/g total natural uranium). pCi/g means picocuries per gram.
  - "Unrefined, unprocessed ore may be exempt above 0.05%."
  — [TCEQ RG-486 Radioactive Material Exemptions](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/downloads/permitting/radioactive-materials/publications/rg-486-radioactive-material-exemptions.pdf)
- An industry summary says ores with ≥0.05% uranium or thorium are source material regulated under federal rule 10 CFR Part 40 by NRC or an Agreement State. It also says "downstream concentration can push process materials over this threshold even if the original ore was below it." — [Rare Earth Exchanges, "Radioactivity in the U.S. Rare Earth Supply Chain"](https://rareearthexchanges.com/?p=90908)
- The same summary says 11e.(2) covers only ore "processed primarily for its source-material content," so rare-earth facilities "are not automatically uranium mills," and their residues may fall outside 11e.(2). — [Rare Earth Exchanges](https://rareearthexchanges.com/?p=90908)

**NORM rule (25 TAC §289.259), administered by DSHS**
- Scope: the rule covers anyone "extracting, mining, beneficiating, processing, using, transferring, transporting, or storing NORM." The NORM definition "does not include source, byproduct, or special nuclear material." — [25 TAC §289.259 (Cornell LII)](https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/25-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-289-259)
- Exemption levels for NORM other than oil-and-gas NORM, §289.259(d)(1)(B):
  - Radium-226 or radium-228 at ≤30 pCi/g, if radon emanation is <20 pCi/m²/s.
  - Radium-226 or radium-228 at ≤5 pCi/g, if radon emanation is ≥20 pCi/m²/s.
  - Any other NORM radionuclide at ≤150 pCi/g.
  — [25 TAC §289.259](https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/25-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-289-259)
- General license, §289.259(f): non-exempt NORM may be mined, processed, stored, and transferred for disposal "without regard to quantity" under a general license, meaning no individual application. A specific license, §289.259(i), is needed for manufacturing or distributing NORM products above the exempt levels, for decontaminating others' equipment or land, and for receiving others' NORM waste. — [25 TAC §289.259](https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/25-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-289-259)
- Disposal, §289.259(h): NORM waste must be disposed of by one of three routes: under EPA requirements, by transfer to a person specifically licensed to receive NORM waste, or by alternate methods the disposal agency authorizes. — [25 TAC §289.259](https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/25-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-289-259)

**TCEQ NORM disposal and exemptions**
- TCEQ says commercial NORM disposal (its example is drinking-water-treatment NORM) is allowed "only by injection into a Class I injection well." That route needs a license under 30 TAC Chapter 336 Subchapter K plus a Class I well permit under 30 TAC Chapter 331. Exempt NORM is regulated as ordinary solid waste. — [TCEQ NORM Waste Disposal](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/permitting/radmat/uranium/norm.html)
- TCEQ's exemption-concurrence process, where TCEQ confirms in writing that a waste meets the exemption rules:
  - It carries no fee and typically takes up to 2 weeks.
  - Averaging is allowed over at most 20 cubic yards, with 4–5 samples per 20 cubic yards.
  - No single measurement may exceed 10 times the exemption criterion.
  - Dilution cannot be used to meet the criteria.
  — [TCEQ RG-486](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/downloads/permitting/radioactive-materials/publications/rg-486-radioactive-material-exemptions.pdf)

**Real Texas precedent for a rare-earth plant licensed for thorium**
- Solvay USA, Freeport, Texas (Texas license L02807):
  - It was a rare-earth separation plant producing hydroxides and nitrates, with roughly 4,000 t/yr rare-earth-oxide capacity.
  - "Thorium and uranium were removed from the ore," and thorium-232 is the main radionuclide of concern.
  - The last unit stopped in 2005. A decommissioning plan was approved 1 September 2015, covering about 17 acres, with a dose-based release criterion of 25 mrem/yr. The closure date was extended to August 2019.
  - The listing page shows "last updated" September 2026.
  — [NRC Complex Materials Sites: Solvay USA (Texas)](https://www.nrc.gov/info-finder/decommissioning/complex/solvay-usa-inc.html)

**Permitting timelines (TCEQ's own statistics, FY2023–2024 averages for uncontested applications)**
- Air NSR new permits: 241 days. NSR amendments: 243 days. The federal-timeline variants are 258 days (new) and 370 days (amendments). — [TCEQ Biennial Report 2023–24, Appendix B](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/downloads/publications/sfr/srf-057-24/biennial-report-23-24-appendix-b_x.pdf)
- Water quality new permits (minor facilities): 155 days. Major amendments: 141–168 days. — [same](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/downloads/publications/sfr/srf-057-24/biennial-report-23-24-appendix-b_x.pdf)
- Industrial and hazardous waste new permits: 509 days. — [same](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/downloads/publications/sfr/srf-057-24/biennial-report-23-24-appendix-b_x.pdf)
- Radioactive-material licences:
  - Uranium minor amendments: 385 days (1 processed).
  - Uranium licence renewals: 862 days (3 processed).
  - No averages exist for new radioactive-material licences, because almost none were processed.
  — [same](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/downloads/publications/sfr/srf-057-24/biennial-report-23-24-appendix-b_x.pdf)
- Public participation adds steps and time:
  - The applicant publishes the Notice of Receipt and Intent (NORI) within 30 days of administrative completeness.
  - After the Notice of Application and Preliminary Decision (NAPD) there is a comment period of at least 30 days.
  - Requests for a contested case hearing are due within 30 days of the decision letter.
  - A granted hearing goes to SOAH as a trial-like proceeding.
  — [TCEQ Public Participation Overview](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/agency/decisions/participation/permitting-participation/overview-public-participation-in-environmental-permitting)

### Inferences
- The classification question decides everything. There are three possible routes:
  1. If the Brazilian fine fraction or concentrate, the MREC, and every residue stay below 0.05% uranium plus thorium, the plant likely runs under the DSHS NORM general license. Radium-bearing residues above 30 pCi/g radium-226/228 or 150 pCi/g of other radionuclides would then go to licensed disposal.
  2. If the feed or MREC reaches ≥0.05% uranium plus thorium, the plant probably needs a TCEQ source-material processing licence. There is little recent Texas precedent for a new one, and TCEQ statistics suggest well over a year.
  3. If thorium is deliberately extracted, residues could arguably become 11e.(2) byproduct. That brings uranium-mill-style tailings rules, a reason to avoid recovering thorium as a product.
- Concentrating rare earths into MREC usually concentrates thorium too, unless thorium is rejected to the leach residue. Either the MREC or the residue (or both) could cross 0.05%. Metallurgical testing must measure thorium and uranium deportment, meaning which stream each element ends up in.
- A realistic Texas schedule for a greenfield acid plant with radioactive residues: about 8–12 months for air and wastewater permits if uncontested; 12–24+ months with contested hearings or a new radioactive-materials licence. These estimates come from the TCEQ averages above plus hearing time.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve TCEQ's specific rules for licensing *source material processing* that is not uranium recovery (30 TAC Chapter 336 subchapters), or any licence fee schedule (TCEQ or DSHS 25 TAC §289.204). Fees are not quantified.
- I could not confirm whether DSHS or TCEQ licensed Solvay Freeport. The NRC page says only that the "Agreement State (Texas)" provided the information.
- No source states how Texas would classify an imported Brazilian concentrate that is ≥0.05% thorium. A pre-application meeting with TCEQ's Radioactive Materials Division (radmat@tceq.texas.gov) and DSHS would be needed.

## Q2. How are the acid-neutralization gypsum and other residues classified under federal and Texas waste rules (phosphogypsum rule Subpart R, Bevill, Texas Class 1/2/3)?

### Takeaway
None of the Tiros acid-process wastes is on the RCRA Bevill list of 20 exempt mineral-processing wastes. That means: the leach residue, the neutralization gypsum, the iron/aluminium hydroxide sludges, and the TiO₂ sulfate-process waste acids. Each must be tested against the hazardous-waste characteristics (corrosivity and leachable metals). If not hazardous, Texas classifies it as Class 1, 2, or 3 industrial waste.

The EPA phosphogypsum rule (40 CFR 61 Subpart R) applies only to gypsum from wet-process phosphoric acid, so it does not directly cover this gypsum. Its 10 pCi/g radium limit for reuse is a useful benchmark only. Radioactivity is handled separately by the Texas NORM and source-material rules.

### Cited Findings
**Bevill exclusion**
- 40 CFR 261.4(b)(7)(ii) limits exempt mineral-processing wastes to 20 named wastes. Examples include red and brown muds from bauxite refining, phosphogypsum from phosphoric acid production, and "(S) Chloride process waste solids from titanium tetrachloride production." There is no entry for sulfate-process TiO₂ wastes or rare-earth wastes. — [eCFR 40 CFR 261.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-I/part-261/subpart-A/section-261.4)
- Beneficiation is defined to include "dissolution," "precipitation," "solvent extraction," "ion exchange," and "roasting ... in preparation for leaching (except where the roasting ... /leaching sequence produces a final or intermediate product that does not undergo further beneficiation or processing)." — [eCFR 40 CFR 261.4(b)(7)(i)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-I/part-261/subpart-A/section-261.4)
- In 1989 EPA's list of 33 conditionally retained wastes included "Sulfate processing waste acids from titanium dioxide production," "Sulfate processing waste solids from titanium dioxide production," "Process wastewater from primary cerium processing," and "Ammonium nitrate process solution from primary lanthanide processing." These were to be re-evaluated. — [EPA, 54 FR 36592 (Sept. 1989)](https://archive.epa.gov/epawaste/nonhaz/industrial/special/web/pdf/54fr36592.pdf)
- None of them appears in the final 20-waste list. — [eCFR 40 CFR 261.4(b)(7)(ii)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-I/part-261/subpart-A/section-261.4)
- EPA states that other mineral-processing wastes "are subject to RCRA Subtitle C if they are listed or exhibit a hazardous characteristic." The Land Disposal Restrictions Phase IV rule (May 1998) "applies to all mineral processing wastes that are discarded, except for 20 specific mineral processing wastes," and the D.C. Circuit upheld use of the Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP), a lab test of how much metal leaches out, for mineral-processing wastes. — [EPA, Mineral Processing Wastes fact sheet](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2013-09/documents/mineral.pdf)
- For context, EPA withdrew chloride-ilmenite TiCl₄ waste acids from the exclusion in 1989 after finding them toxic for chromium. DuPont challenged the withdrawal. — [EPA TiO₂ report summary](https://archive.epa.gov/epawaste/nonhaz/industrial/special/web/txt/tio2_ex.txt)

**Phosphogypsum rule (40 CFR 61 Subpart R)**
- The rule defines phosphogypsum as "the solid waste byproduct which results from the process of wet acid phosphorus production" and applies to "each owner or operator of a phosphogypsum stack." An inactive stack may not emit more than 20 pCi/(m²·s) of radon. Agricultural use requires radium-226 ≤10 pCi/g. — [eCFR 40 CFR 61 Subpart R](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-61/subpart-R)

**Texas industrial waste classes (30 TAC 335 Subchapter R)**
- Industrial generators must classify non-hazardous waste as Class 1, 2, or 3. Out-of-state generators shipping into Texas are also bound. — [WTS, Waste Classification in Texas](https://wtsonline.com/waste-classification-in-texas/)
- Class 1 (§335.505) includes:
  - Wastes with leachable constituents at or above the §335.521 table.
  - Solids or semi-solids giving pH ≤2 or ≥12.5 when mixed 1:1 with water.
  - Wastes lacking data or process knowledge to prove Class 2 or 3.
  Class 1 waste must go to a landfill permitted for hazardous/Class 1 waste and be manifested. — [WTS](https://wtsonline.com/waste-classification-in-texas/); [30 TAC §335.505](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/30-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-335-505)
- Class 3 is "inert, non-liquid and essentially insoluble" (rock, brick, glass, dirt). Class 2 is everything non-hazardous that is neither Class 1 nor Class 3. Class 1 is "more expensive to dispose of," and fewer landfills accept it. — [TCEQ Waste Matrix Q42](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/assistance/waste/waste-matrix/matrixq42.html); [WTS](https://wtsonline.com/waste-classification-in-texas/)
- Texas state waste management fees (per ton, effective 1 September 2023): commercial Class 1 landfill $7.38 (in-state) or $9.23 (out-of-state); commercial hazardous landfill $36.90 or $46.13. These are state fees on top of the landfill's own charges. — [TCEQ IHW Management Fees FY24](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/downloads/permitting/waste-permits/ihw/docs/ihw-management-fees-fy24.pdf)

**Radioactivity of rare-earth residues**
- EPA: "Processing rare earth minerals involves the separation and removal of uranium and thorium, which results in TENORM wastes." — [EPA TENORM: Rare Earths Mining Wastes](https://epa.gov/radiation/tenorm-rare-earths-mining-wastes)

### Inferences
Expected classification of each waste stream:

| Stream | Likely Texas class | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Acid-bake/leach residue (silica, unreacted minerals) | Class 1 or Class 2 | Class 1 if residual acidity or leachable metals remain. Could fall to Class 2 after washing and neutralization. Probably carries most of the radium and possibly thorium, so may also be NORM. |
| Neutralization gypsum | Class 2 or possibly Class 3 | If well neutralized and low in leachable metals. Co-precipitated radium could exceed 30 pCi/g, which would make it non-exempt NORM even though Subpart R doesn't apply. |
| Iron/aluminium hydroxide sludge | Class 1 or Class 2 | Depends on leachable metal content. |

- Sale of gypsum (to cement or wallboard) would need radium data, and buyers commonly look to the 10 pCi/g Subpart R benchmark.
- The "roasting ... in preparation for leaching" wording does not help. The acid bake/leach makes an intermediate product (the TiO₂ hydrolysis feed and the MREC) that is further processed, and the plant's acid steps are chemical processing, not beneficiation.

### Gaps
- I found no EPA determination specifically on sulfate-process TiO₂ waste acids after 1989 or 1990 (the 1990 final rule, 55 FR 32135, was not retrieved). Their final non-Bevill status is inferred from the current 20-item list.
- There is no public data on leachable metals or radioactivity in Tiros residues. Test work is required.

## Q3. Real US precedents (Lynas Seadrift, Energy Fuels White Mesa, MP Materials Mountain Pass, Round Top, TiO₂ pigment plants)

### Takeaway
Lynas's Seadrift, Texas plant is the most relevant precedent, and it failed largely on wastewater:
- It got up to US$258M of Department of Defense money in 2023.
- It stalled over "unresolved wastewater disposal permit issues" through 2025.
- In its 26 August 2026 FY26 annual report, Lynas confirmed the Texas heavy-rare-earth facility "will no longer proceed" and that the US funding was reallocated to a supply agreement.

Energy Fuels' White Mesa mill (Utah) shows the opposite model: put monazite processing inside an existing uranium-mill licence with licensed tailings cells.

### Cited Findings
**Lynas, Seadrift (Calhoun County, Texas)**
- Site: a 149-acre greenfield site in Seadrift bought from Dow Chemical. The input was MREC pre-processed at Kalgoorlie (Western Australia) from Mt Weld ore. Steps shown were cracking and leaching, solvent extraction, and product finishing.
- Funding: about US$258M Department of Defense grant for the heavy-rare-earth plant plus about US$30M matched for light rare earths.
- Target: operational between July 2025 and June 2026.
- The open-house slides named no permits or waste plan.
— [Lynas Open House presentation, 7 Dec 2023](https://lynasrareearths.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Lynas-Rare-Earths_Open-House-Presentation-December-7-2023.pdf)
- April 2025: wastewater management issues raised costs. CEO Amanda Lacaze said the cost rise was "sufficiently meaningful for us to not want to carry the risk," and that Seadrift was "not a critical path item for us in the same way it is for the U.S. government." — [North American Mining (summarising WSJ), 30 Apr 2025](https://northamericanmining.com/index.php/2025/04/30/lynas-seeks-u-s-help-with-costs-of-texas-project/)
- August 2025: Lynas flagged "uncertainty" over the Texas plant. — [Mining Weekly, 28 Aug 2025](https://www.miningweekly.com/article/lynas-flags-uncertainty-over-texas-rare-earths-plant-posts-profit-slump-2025-08-28)
- 26 November 2025 annual general meeting: Chairman John Humphrey said "no progress had been made" in recent months. The plant was "unlikely to proceed" due to "unresolved wastewater disposal permit issues." Planned capacity was 2,500–3,000 t/yr heavy and 5,000 t/yr light rare earths. — [SMM, 28 Nov 2025](https://news.metal.com/newscontent/103646309-lynas-texas-rare-earth-plant-plan-stalled-over-wastewater-permit-issues); [The Metalnomist, Feb 2026](https://www.metalnomist.com/2026/02/lynas-texas-rare-earth-processing-plant.html)
- Lynas FY26 Annual Report, released 26 August 2026: it says Lynas "signed a binding Letter of Intent with the U.S. Government in March 2026," and that "Funding for the supply agreement has been reallocated from funds previously intended ... for the construction of a HRE facility in Texas which will no longer proceed." — [Lynas FY26 Annual Report (ASX, 26 Aug 2026)](https://announcements.asx.com.au/asxpdf/20260826/pdf/0737nx07s3tnf7.pdf)
- The March 2026 letter of intent covers about US$96M of oxide purchases over four years. — [Rare Earth Directory, Lynas USA (verified Oct 2026)](https://rareearthdirectory.com/rare-earth-companies-in-the-us/lynas-usa/)
- Local context: Seadrift is also home to a long-running fight over Dow and Formosa wastewater discharges to the bays, led by activist Diane Wilson. This is evidence that bay discharge permits are heavily scrutinized there. — [Inside Climate News, Mar 2026](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/02032026/dow-requests-texas-plastic-pollution-permit/)

**Energy Fuels, White Mesa Mill (San Juan County, Utah)**
- In November 2020 Energy Fuels produced rare-earth concentrate from monazite sand. It planned to import at least 2,500 tons/yr of monazite from Georgia for three years.
- The Utah Division of Waste Management and Radiation Control agreed the monazite qualified as "natural ore" under the mill's existing licence, based on a company letter.
- Activist Sarah Fields challenged this, saying monazite should need a separate licence, which would mean "months" plus public comment.
— [KUER, 16 Dec 2020](https://www.kuer.org/health-science-environment/2020-12-16/plans-to-process-radioactive-sand-in-san-juan-county-raise-questions-about-state-oversight)
- Energy Fuels describes White Mesa as "the only uranium mill capable of producing separated REEs, in the U.S. today." Its proposed Phase 2 rare-earth circuit is "subject to the receipt of regulatory approvals." — [Energy Fuels 10-K FY2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1385849/000138584926000009/efr-20251231.htm)
- White Mesa operates under uranium-mill standards. Mountain Pass is regulated through California state environmental and radiation-control mechanisms. — [Rare Earth Exchanges](https://rareearthexchanges.com/?p=90908)

**MP Materials, Mountain Pass (California)**
- The flowsheet includes "tailings thickening and filtration followed by dry stack tailings disposal." About 5.3 million short tons of tailings capacity has been used and about 16.3 million short tons (roughly 18 years) remain, with a northwest expansion around 2043. Evaporation ponds are listed among existing facilities. — [MP Materials Technical Report Summary, 10-K FY2025 Ex. 96.1](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1801368/000180136826000008/mpmcexhibit961123125.htm)

**Round Top (Hudspeth County, Texas)**
- USA Rare Earth holds mining rights there. Processing would need evaluation based on uranium and thorium concentrations. — [Rare Earth Exchanges](https://rareearthexchanges.com/?p=90908)

### Inferences
- Lynas Seadrift shows that in coastal Texas the *wastewater* permit (dissolved salts, ammonium/sulfate, metals to a bay) can be the binding constraint, more than radioactive licensing. The Tiros plan should assume zero liquid discharge or evaporation and crystallization, or a deep-well injection option, and budget for it.
- The White Mesa route (an existing licensed uranium mill with tailings cells) is the low-permitting-risk way to handle thorium-bearing residues in the US. A Brownsville greenfield site lacks that.
- Lynas planned to receive *already-cracked* MREC, keeping the radioactive cracking residues in Australia/Malaysia. By contrast, the Tiros plan would generate the acid-bake residue, and with it most radioactivity, in Texas.

### Gaps
- I did not find Lynas's TCEQ permit numbers, its proposed discharge composition, or which permit was blocked.
- I did not retrieve details of Round Top permitting, Texas uranium-mill licences, or Chemours/Tronox/Kronos TiO₂ pigment acid-waste handling. Kronos and Tronox are chloride-process in the US. This should be checked in a later pass.
- I did not retrieve Mountain Pass radioactive-licence details or closure financial assurance figures.

## Q4. Disposal options and costs in Texas (low-level radioactive waste, NORM, and industrial landfill)

### Takeaway
Texas has one commercial radioactive-waste disposal complex: WCS in Andrews County, about 600+ miles by road from Brownsville. It takes 11e.(2) byproduct material, NORM/TENORM, and exempt waste, but publishes no prices. Non-hazardous Class 2 residues can go to commercial landfills. The state Class 1 fee is about US$7–9 per ton plus the gate fee. Published per-tonne prices for radioactive or NORM disposal are scarce and dated.

### Cited Findings
- WCS (Andrews County) operates:
  - The Texas Compact Waste Facility, for Class A/B/C LLRW, since 2012.
  - The Federal Waste Facility, since 2013.
  - A Byproduct Facility that "accepts 11.e(2) byproduct material."
  - A Hazardous/TSCA/Exempt Waste Facility. A TCEQ-approved exemption process there allows disposal of roughly 10% of the Class A limit.
  Andrews County gets 5% of gross receipts and the state 26.25% of disposal gross receipts. — [WCS Media Backgrounder](https://www.wcstexas.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/WCS_Media-Backgrounder.pdf); [WCS facilities](https://www.wcstexas.com/about/our-facilities/)
- WCS lists accepted waste types as Class A, B, and C waste, mixed and low-level waste, NORM, TENORM, RCRA/TSCA waste, and byproduct material. — [WCS Our Facilities](https://www.wcstexas.com/about/our-facilities/)
- Historical NORM disposal costs (early 1998 data, published January 1999):

### Inferences (estimates, not sourced prices)
- Scale: at about 5,000 t/month of run-of-mine (ROM, i.e. raw mined ore) equivalent feed, the residue would be thousands of tonnes per month of leach residue plus gypsum. Shipping that to WCS by rail or truck (~1,000 km) and paying radioactive-disposal rates would likely cost hundreds of dollars per tonne. That is economically prohibitive for bulk residue.
- Design goal: keep bulk residue at or below the NORM exemption levels (≤30 pCi/g radium-226/228; <0.05% uranium plus thorium). Then send it as Class 2 industrial waste to landfill, at a placeholder of roughly US$40–100 per tonne including the state fee. Isolate only a small thorium/radium-rich cut, such as a radium-barium sulfate co-precipitate, for WCS.
- These cost ranges are planning placeholders. Get written quotes from WCS and from Gulf Coast Class 1/Class 2 landfills.

## Q5. Brownsville-specific factors

### Takeaway
The Port of Brownsville has industrial land and heavy-industry neighbours: Rio Grande LNG, Texas LNG, a proposed refinery, and SpaceX nearby. It also has an organized, litigious environmental-justice opposition (the South Texas Environmental Justice Network, the Carrizo/Comecrudo Tribe, the City of Port Isabel, Sierra Club). That opposition won a 2024 D.C. Circuit vacatur of the LNG approvals, which the federal energy regulator later re-issued. Water supply from the Lower Rio Grande is tight: in 2024 there were reservoir lows, a Cameron County disaster declaration, and the closure of Texas's last sugar mill for lack of water. The coast is hurricane-exposed.

### Cited Findings
- On 6 August 2024 the D.C. Circuit overturned the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) authorizations for Rio Grande LNG, Texas LNG, and the Rio Bravo Pipeline. It found FERC "erroneously declined" to fully assess air-quality impacts on nearby marginalized communities. The challengers were Sierra Club, the Carrizo/Comecrudo Tribe of Texas, the City of Port Isabel, and others. — [Canary Media, 8 Aug 2024](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/liquefied-natural-gas/a-us-court-struck-down-permits-for-controversial-lng-projects-in-texas)
- Earlier remand round: FERC reauthorized both LNG projects on 21 April 2023 by a 3–1 vote, after about 20 months of supplemental review. It used a 50 km environmental-justice radius and added air monitoring of fine particulates (PM2.5, PM10) and nitrogen dioxide. — [Van Ness Feldman, 25 Apr 2023](https://www.vnf.com/ferc-reauthorizes-texas-lng-rio-grande-lng-projects)
- Later events: FERC issued a further reauthorization order in 2025 ([Venable, Oct 2025](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2025/10/fercs-new-lng-order-reflects-trump-admin)). In May 2026 opponents were preparing new litigation ([Oil & Gas 360, 29 May 2026](https://www.oilandgas360.com/opponents-inch-closer-to-filing-lawsuit-over-port-of-brownsville-lng-terminal/)). *(Both are headline-level only; content not fully verified.)*
- March 2026: President Trump announced "America First Refining," a 160,000 barrel/day refinery at the port. It relies on a 2021 TCEQ air permit (originally issued to Jupiter Brownsville). Shrimpers, environmental groups, and the Carrizo Comecrudo tribe objected to the permit. The South Texas Environmental Justice Network "has opposed the refinery for over seven years," and LULAC raised the region's water shortage. — [The Border Chronicle, 12 Mar 2026](https://www.theborderchronicle.com/trump-announces-first-new-u-s-refinery-in-50-years-at-port-of-brownsville-reviving-local-resistance/)
- Water (April 2024):
  - Combined Amistad/Falcon reservoir supply was at 22%, below McAllen's 25% Stage 2 trigger.
  - Mexico was more than 700,000 acre-feet behind on 1944 Treaty deliveries (the cycle ended October 2025).
  - Cameron County Judge Treviño issued a drought disaster declaration on 15 April 2024.
  - Texas's last sugar mill closed for lack of water.
  — [Texas Tribune, 18 Apr 2024](https://www.texastribune.org/2024/04/18/south-texas-drought-agriculture/)
- Hurricane exposure: the port went to Condition Yankee and later Zulu (hurricane readiness levels) for Hurricane Beryl in July 2024. — [Port of Brownsville](https://www.portofbrownsville.com/hurricane-beryl-update-port-of-brownsville-under-condition-yankee/)

### Inferences
- A radioactive-residue acid plant at the port would almost certainly draw contested-case hearing requests from the same coalition that fought the LNG projects. Plan on contested TCEQ hearings, adding roughly 6–12+ months, and a community engagement program.
- Water-intensive steps (leaching, washing, gypsum slurry) compete with severe drought. Plan for water recycling, a reclaimed or brackish source, and ideally zero liquid discharge, especially given Lynas's experience.
- Waste storage on site (gypsum stacks, residue cells) in a hurricane and storm-surge zone needs engineered containment above surge elevation. Dry stacking is strongly preferable to ponds.

## Q6. Brazil (Minas Gerais) comparison

### Takeaway
In Brazil the residues would stay at or near Tiros. The radioactive side is governed by the national nuclear regulator's mining standard (CNEN NN 4.01, the Norma for "Instalações Mínero-Industriais," i.e. mining-industrial facilities). That standard sets facility categories at 500 Bq/g and 10 Bq/g of specific activity (radioactivity per gram), with a simplified safety report for mid-range facilities. Environmental licensing is by state agencies (Minas Gerais SEMAD/COPAM) using a staged preliminary/installation/operating licence sequence. A new federal General Environmental Licensing Law (Law 15,190/2025) takes effect in February 2026. Minas Gerais post-Brumadinho dam law strongly disfavours new tailings dams, pushing toward filtered and dry-stacked residues.

### Cited Findings
- The current version of CNEN/ANSN NN 4.01 is Resolution CNEN 208/2016, published 26 December 2016. ANSN, the new National Nuclear Safety Authority, now lists it as "Norma ANSN 4.01." The same group of norms includes Resolution 179/2014 on phosphogypsum use in agriculture and cement. — [gov.br CNEN/ANSN Group 4 norms](https://www.gov.br/cnen/pt-br/acesso-rapido/normas/grupo-4)
- NN 4.01 (2002 base text; categories presumed similar in the 2016 version):
  - Category I: >500 Bq/g, or dose >1 mSv/yr. Requires a full safety analysis report (RAS).
  - Category II: 10–500 Bq/g. Requires a simplified report (RASS).
  - Category III: <10 Bq/g with worker dose >1 mSv/yr. Requires only preliminary information.
  - Approval is valid for 2 years, renewed automatically.
  - A waste management plan with leaching tests (Brazilian standards NBR 10005/10006) is required.
  - Effluent doses >0.3 mSv/yr to the critical group trigger treatment.
  — [CNEN NN 4.01 base text (2002)](https://repositorio.mctic.gov.br/bitstream/mctic/5130/1/2002_nn_401_requisitos_de_seguranca_e_protecao_radiologica_para_instalacoes_minero-industriais.pdf)
- Law 15,190/2025 (General Environmental Licensing Law):
  - Sanctioned 8 August 2025 with 63 vetoes. Congress overrode most vetoes on 27 November 2025.
  - It creates a Special Environmental License (LAE) for "strategic" projects. The LAE still requires an EIA/RIMA (the Brazilian environmental impact study and its public summary) and three licensing phases.
  - The self-declaration licence (LAC) is barred for mining.
  — [Mayer Brown, Dec 2025](https://www.mayerbrown.com/pt/insights/publications/2025/12/brazilian-congress-overrides-presidential-vetoes-to-the-general-environmental-licensing-law-and-approves-new-rules-on-the-special-environmental-license)
- The law enters into force in February 2026, with licence types LP, LI, LO, LAU, LAC, and LAE. — [Lefosse](https://lefosse.com/noticias/alerta/lei-geral-do-licenciamento-ambiental-o-que-muda-com-a-lei-no-15-190-2025/)
- Minas Gerais Law 23,291/2019 ("Mar de Lama Nunca Mais," "Never Again a Sea of Mud"):
  - It bars licences to build, expand, or raise a dam where failure studies show a community in the self-rescue zone, meaning the area closest downstream where residents must evacuate themselves.
  - It requires proof of "best available technique and alternative location" for tailings in dams.
  - It requires financial guarantees.
  - Decree 48,747/2023 reduced the decommissioning bond, and critics call it insufficient.
  — [Earthworks, Global Benchmarking: Brazil (Nov 2025)](https://earthworks.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Global-Benchmarking-of-Mining-Waste-Rules-–-Brazil.pdf)
- Federal Law 14,066/2020 amended the National Dam Safety Policy, and the federal mining agency (ANM) required upstream dams to be decommissioned by 25 February 2022, with extensions allowed. Minas Gerais required upstream dams to be decommissioned by February 2022. — [Earthworks](https://earthworks.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Global-Benchmarking-of-Mining-Waste-Rules-–-Brazil.pdf); [Machado Meyer](https://www.machadomeyer.com.br/en/recent-publications/press-ij/recent-developments-in-brazilian-mining-regulations-licensing-tailings-dams-and-mine-closures)
- Brazilian Supreme Court challenges were filed against Law 15,190 when it took effect, per a headline. — [Monitor Mercantil](https://monitormercantil.com.br/lei-do-licenciamento-ambiental-entra-em-vigor-com-acoes-no-stf/) *(headline only)*

### Inferences
- Processing in Minas Gerais keeps residues next to the mine and avoids shipping thorium-bearing material internationally. That removes the import classification question and Texas radioactive-waste disposal costs.
- The trade-off: dam-law constraints favour filtered or dry-stack residue storage at Tiros, and CNEN approval would be needed as a Category II facility if residues fall between 10 and 500 Bq/g.
- Brazilian staged licensing for a mining-industrial project commonly takes multiple years. Law 15,190's deadlines may shorten this, but they are untested and legally contested.

- I did not obtain statutory maximum review deadlines (months) under Law 15,190/2025, or typical SEMAD processing times.

- I did not find CNEN rules on *exporting* thorium-bearing concentrate (the Group 4 resolutions 04/69, 08/77, and 18/88 on exporters of nuclear-element-bearing minerals exist but were not read). That is relevant if shipping fines to Texas.

## Q7. Overall timeline and cost estimate (synthesis, mostly estimates)

### Takeaway
Texas: about 1–2 years for air, wastewater, storm water, and waste registration if uncontested. Two to three or more years if a new TCEQ source-material licence and contested hearings are involved. Lynas shows wastewater can block a project outright. Brazil: residues stay at the mine under CNEN NN 4.01 and state licensing, likely multi-year as well, but with no radioactive-waste export or import and no long-haul disposal.
