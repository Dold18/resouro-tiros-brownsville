# Shipping fine (<75 µm), clay-rich, moist mineral concentrate (and sulfur/acid) from Minas Gerais, Brazil to the Port of Brownsville, Texas

Research date: 4 November 2026. All prices are labelled with the date of the source. "Inferences" are my own reasoning or arithmetic from the cited inputs, not sourced facts. Abbreviations used throughout:
- IMSBC Code = International Maritime Solid Bulk Cargoes Code (the mandatory rulebook for carrying solid bulk cargo on ships, under the SOLAS safety convention)
- Group A = cargo that may liquefy (turn into a slurry and shift) if shipped too wet; Group B = chemically hazardous; Group C = neither
- TML = Transportable Moisture Limit (the highest moisture content considered safe to ship in bulk); FMP = Flow Moisture Point (the moisture at which a lab sample starts to flow)
- FIBC = flexible intermediate bulk container, i.e. a 1–1.5 t woven "jumbo bag" or "bulk bag"
- ROM = run-of-mine ore (ore as mined, before any processing)
- MREC = mixed rare-earth carbonate (the rare-earth intermediate product)
- Handysize ≈ 25–40 kt deadweight bulk carrier; Supramax/Ultramax ≈ 50–64 kt
- t = metric tonne; kt = thousand tonnes; Mt = million tonnes; nm = nautical miles

## 1. IMSBC Code, Group A cargoes and liquefaction: would a fine anatase/kaolinite concentrate be Group A, and how is such material normally shipped?

### Takeaway
A wet, 100%-passing-75-µm anatase + kaolinite concentrate should be planned as a Group A (liquefiable) cargo unless laboratory testing proves otherwise: the IMSBC Code explicitly says damp cargo containing fine particles must be flow-tested, clays have liquefied at sea despite the generic "Clay" entry being Group C, and liquefaction is the single biggest killer of bulk-carrier crews. To ship in bulk it must be below its TML (set by lab test, typically 90% of the Flow Moisture Point), which for a clay-rich filter cake almost certainly means thermal drying; bagging in FIBCs takes it outside the bulk-cargo rules but does not remove the physical risk if the material is wet.

### Cited Findings
- Group A cargoes under the IMSBC Code can liquefy when moisture exceeds their TML; examples include iron ore fines, nickel ore and mineral concentrates. Liquefaction turns a solid into a nearly fluid mass that can shift and capsize a ship — [Swedish Club, Cargo Advice: Liquefaction (Dec 2025)](https://www.swedishclub.com/uploads/2025/12/Cargo-Advice-Liquefaction.pdf)
- Definitions: FMP = moisture content (wet-mass basis) at which a flow state forms under the prescribed test; TML = maximum moisture considered safe for carriage in ships without special cargo-confinement provisions. For the Flow Table and Penetration tests, TML = 90% of FMP (10% margin for sampling/testing uncertainty). For the Proctor/Fagerberg test, TML = moisture at 70% degree of saturation. Modified Proctor/Fagerberg procedures exist for iron ore fines, coal and bauxite — [Swedish Club (Dec 2025)](https://www.swedishclub.com/uploads/2025/12/Cargo-Advice-Liquefaction.pdf)
- The "can test" is a simple onboard/field check; free moisture at the end means lab testing is needed. It is not quantitative and a pass does not prove moisture is below TML — [Swedish Club (Dec 2025)](https://www.swedishclub.com/uploads/2025/12/Cargo-Advice-Liquefaction.pdf)
- Pre-loading paperwork for Group A: valid TML certificate (less than 6 months old; reissued if composition changes), moisture certificate from stockpile sampling no more than 7 days before loading, Cargo Declaration Form, and a certificate from the loading-port Competent Authority approving the shipper's sampling and moisture-control procedures (Section 4.3.3, "often overlooked"). Stockpiles should be covered; loading suspended in rain; bilge soundings taken daily — [Swedish Club (Dec 2025)](https://www.swedishclub.com/uploads/2025/12/Cargo-Advice-Liquefaction.pdf)
- IMSBC Appendix 3: "Many fine-particled cargoes if possessing a sufficiently high moisture content are liable to flow"; any damp cargo containing fine particles should be tested for flow characteristics before loading — [Gard (18 Sep 2017)](https://gard.no/en/insights/update-on-group-bauxite-that-can-cause-vessel-instability); same Appendix 3 point in [Hill Dickinson](https://www.hilldickinson.com/our-view/articles/cargo-liquefaction-and-cargo-classification-group-a-or-group-c-cargo/)
- Most Group A cargoes are mineral concentrates. Nickel ore, bauxite fines and iron ore fines were added to Group A only after ships were lost (nickel ore was previously unlisted; iron ore and bauxite were Group C). Chrome concentrate declared Group C has liquefied on passage. Code schedules are guidance and not exhaustive; shippers carry responsibility for correct declaration and testing; competent-authority approval "can take a long time and may force a voyage to be abandoned" — [Hill Dickinson (2025)](https://www.hilldickinson.com/our-view/articles/cargo-liquefaction-and-cargo-classification-group-a-or-group-c-cargo/)
- **Clays specifically:** the generic IMSBC "Clay" entry is Group C, but this "does not necessarily apply to all clays." A full bulk cargo of Malaysian Ball Clay (a kaolinite clay) liquefied and listed 15° en route to Chittagong; another Ball Clay ship from the same port that month was abandoned with significant loss of life. A spot sample was ~9 percentage points above its TML. The IMSBC Code classes "Ilmenite Clay" as Group A (a ship carrying it was nearly lost off Finland in Nov 2000), and the predecessor Code listed "Kaolinite Clay" with a TML — [West P&I Safety Alert SA018 (undated, ~2013)](https://www.westpandi.com/globalassets/loss-prevention/loss-prevention-safety-alerts/131125-sa018---cargo-liquefaction-incident---ball-clay-from-malaysia.pdf)
- **Bulk Jupiter and bauxite:** the Supramax Bulk Jupiter sank in January 2015 carrying bauxite with the loss of 18 lives — [UK P&I Club (2021)](https://www.ukpandi.com/news-and-resources/articles/2021/carriage-of-bauxite-which-may-liquefy). The Global Bauxite Working Group found Group A bauxite fails by "dynamic separation" (water and fines rise to form a slurry with a free surface on top of the cargo) rather than classic liquefaction — [Gard (2017)](https://gard.no/en/insights/update-on-group-bauxite-that-can-cause-vessel-instability)
- Bauxite Group A criteria (IMO CCC.1/Circ.2/Rev.1, 20 Sep 2017): bauxite is Group A if >30% of particles are <1 mm AND >40% are <2.5 mm; TML from Modified Proctor/Fagerberg = moisture at 80% saturation (if optimum moisture occurs at ≥90% saturation) or 70% saturation otherwise; free-draining material that never reaches 70% saturation is not liable to liquefy — [IMO CCC.1/Circ.2/Rev.1](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/MediaCentre/PressBriefings/Documents/CCC.1-Circ.2-Rev.1%20-%20Carriage%20Of%20Bauxite%20Which%20May%20Liquefy.pdf). The revised Code with the bauxite schedules entered into force 1 Jan 2021 — [UK P&I Club (2021)](https://www.ukpandi.com/news-and-resources/articles/2021/carriage-of-bauxite-which-may-liquefy)
- Casualty statistics: INTERCARGO's 2013–2022 report: 26 bulk carriers (>10,000 dwt) lost, 104 seafarers dead; cargo liquefaction caused 5 of the losses and 70 of the deaths (4 nickel ore, 1 bauxite) — [gCaptain (Jul/Dec 2023)](https://gcaptain.com/intercargo-warns-against-complacency-as-liquefaction-risk-looms-over-dry-bulk-shipping)
- Copper concentrate example (filtration context): a saleable concentrate needed ≤9% moisture to meet its TML, whereas the filter cake without air-blowing would hold ~18.9% w/w — i.e. TML-driven moisture targets can be about half of what a filter delivers — [Metso, Beginner's guide to concentrate filtration](https://www.metso.com/insights/blog/mining-and-metals/beginners-guide-to-concentrate-filtration/)
- **Bags (FIBCs):** "Jumbo bags are not considered as bulk cargo"; shippers may bag to avoid IMSBC requirements for Group A or B cargo; the IMDG Code (dangerous goods in packages) then applies if the cargo has a UN number. Group A cargo bagged above its FMP can deform and compress, bags shift, and shifting bags across several decks have caused severe lists. Gard recommends still consulting the IMSBC Code, compartmentalising the stow and checking bag stacking load — [Gard, Bulk cargo in jumbo bags (23 Jul 2025)](https://gard.no/insights/bulk-cargo-in-jumbo-bags-understanding-the-risks/)

### Inferences
- The Tiros fine fraction (100% <75 µm, weathered anatase + REE minerals + kaolinite) is far finer than any bauxite Group A threshold (which is set at 1 mm and 2.5 mm). A competent authority, P&I club or shipowner would almost certainly require it to be tested and declared as Group A ("mineral concentrate") unless a TML test shows otherwise. Planning assumption: **treat as Group A.**
- Which TML test applies is itself a test-programme question: the Flow Table Test is the standard method for fine concentrates; Proctor/Fagerberg is often used for coarser material. Very fine, cohesive, plastic clay-rich material can behave oddly in both (it may hold a lot of water before flowing, but it also drains very poorly, so moisture cannot "drain off" during the voyage). The FMP/TML could plausibly be anywhere from ~10% to >25% moisture for this material — this is a guess and must be measured; **add a TML (Flow Table + Proctor/Fagerberg) test to the priority test list.**
- Typical industry approach for a fine Group A concentrate: (1) filter, then (2) dry to a few percentage points below TML and ship in bulk with certificates, or (3) dry further and ship in FIBCs/containers, or (4) ship in sealed containers (a container is not a ship's hold; liquefaction inside a container does not threaten ship stability in the same way, which is why containerising wet concentrates is common practice — this last point is my understanding, not confirmed by a source fetched here).
- Practical consequence: shipping the fines as-is (filter cake, probably 20–30% moisture) in bulk is very unlikely to be allowed; option (a) "wet concentrate in bulk" really becomes "partly dried concentrate below TML in bulk."
- Because 5,000 t/month is small for bulk shipping, a 30–40 kt Handysize cargo means holding 6–8 months of production in a **covered** stockpile at the Brazilian port (to keep moisture below TML and avoid re-testing after rain) — a significant cost and logistics item.

### Gaps
- No published TML/FMP values were found for anatase/kaolinite or any titanium-clay concentrate; must be tested.
- Who the Brazilian "Competent Authority" is for IMSBC approvals (likely the Brazilian Navy's Diretoria de Portos e Costas) was not confirmed.
- No source fetched confirming the P&I/industry view on containerised Group A concentrates (the inference above is from general knowledge).

## 2. Filtering and drying the fines: achievable cake moisture, thermal drying energy and cost, dust and handling

### Takeaway
Clay-rich <75 µm material is hard to filter: vacuum filters only work well down to ~25–30 µm mean particle size, so pressure filters / filter presses are needed, and flaky clay particles blind cloths and hold water. Expect filter cake in roughly the 18–30% moisture range (estimate; not measured). Thermal drying needs ~2.26 GJ per tonne of water in theory and ~3.2–4.5 GJ/t water in practice; drying from ~25% to ~10% moisture costs roughly 0.7–0.9 GJ per dry tonne of concentrate.

### Cited Findings
- Vacuum filters are suitable down to a mean particle size of about 25–30 µm at 0.8 bar differential pressure; pressure filters extend to about 2.5 µm at 10 bar. Clay gangue's plate-like particles create tighter pores and raise retained water; fines migrating to the cloth can form an impermeable layer and blind it; flocculants/coagulants help — [Metso, Beginner's guide to concentrate filtration](https://www.metso.com/insights/blog/mining-and-metals/beginners-guide-to-concentrate-filtration/)
- Without air "gas displacement," a copper concentrate cake retained ~18.9% moisture, roughly double its 9% TML — [Metso](https://www.metso.com/insights/blog/mining-and-metals/beginners-guide-to-concentrate-filtration/)
- Flotation concentrate filter cakes historically run from 8% to 30% moisture; dryers typically remove about half to three-quarters of that moisture. Rotary dryers have thermal efficiencies of 50–70%; rabble dryers ~50% at full load and as low as 30% at reduced load — [911Metallurgist, BTU energy required to dry ores and concentrates](https://www.911metallurgist.com/btu-energy-required-dry-ores-concentrates)
- Rotary dryer sizing example: evaporation heat ~2,257 kJ/kg water; total burner input of 1,250–1,300 kW for 1,225 kg/h water evaporated, i.e. ~3,670–3,820 kJ/kg water (~59–61% implied efficiency; derived by the summarizer from the article's numbers) — [Industrial Monitor Direct, rotary dryer burner sizing](https://industrialmonitordirect.com/blogs/knowledgebase/rotary-dryer-burner-sizing-heat-input-calculation-method)
- Niobium tailings study (Paste 2025): finer tailings gave higher cake moisture; ultrafines <10 µm reduce filtration rates and the authors suggest limiting ultrafines to ~10% for efficient vacuum filtration — [Testa et al., Paste 2025](https://papers.acg.uwa.edu.au/p/2555_08_Testa/)

### Inferences
- **Energy per tonne of water evaporated:** theory 2.26 GJ/t; practical 3.2–4.5 GJ/t (2.26 ÷ 0.70 to 2.26 ÷ 0.50). The task's suggested 2.5–3.5 GJ/t is optimistic for a rotary dryer; use ~3.7 GJ/t as the base case and 3.0 GJ/t for an efficient flash/fluid-bed dryer with heat recovery (estimate).
- **Worked example (my arithmetic, wet-basis moisture):** per 1 dry tonne of solids —
  - cake at 25% moisture contains 0.333 t water; at 10% → 0.111 t; at 2% → 0.020 t.
  - Drying 25% → 10% evaporates 0.222 t water → 0.67–1.0 GJ (base ~0.82 GJ) per dry tonne.
  - Drying 25% → 2% (for bagging/air-tight powder) evaporates 0.313 t → 0.94–1.4 GJ (base ~1.16 GJ) per dry tonne.
  - Drying 30% → 10% evaporates 0.317 t → ~1.17 GJ per dry tonne (base).
- **Fuel cost (assumption, not sourced):** at a natural-gas/LPG/biomass delivered price of US$8–15/GJ in Minas Gerais, fuel alone is roughly US$7–17 per dry tonne; adding power, labour, maintenance and dryer capital recovery, a total drying cost in the order of **US$12–30 per dry tonne** is a reasonable planning range (estimate — needs vendor quote).
- At 60–120 kt/yr dry solids, drying 25%→10% removes ~13–27 kt/yr of water and needs ~50–100 TJ/yr of heat (≈1.6–3.2 MW average thermal load) (my arithmetic).
- Shipping wet also means paying freight on water: at 25% moisture, each dry tonne shipped carries 0.33 t of water (33% freight penalty); at 10%, 0.11 t (11%).
- **Dust and handling:** dried <75 µm clay-rich powder will be very dusty (dust losses during grab discharge and conveying, and possible export/import environmental permit issues), may cake/re-absorb moisture, and can "bridge" in hoppers. Fully dry powder is better suited to sealed FIBCs, containers or pneumatic (cement-type) handling than to open grab-discharge bulk. Partly dried material (~8–12%) is less dusty but nearer the TML. Planning dust/handling loss assumption: 0.5–2% for open bulk handling of dry fines vs <0.5% for bagged/containerised (estimate; no sourced figure found).

### Gaps
- No sourced cake-moisture figure specifically for kaolinite-rich <75 µm concentrates with pressure filters (needed: filtration test work with flocculant screening).
- No sourced drying-cost (US$/t) or dust-loss-percentage benchmarks for fine mineral powders; vendor (FLSmidth/Metso/Andritz) quotes needed.

## 3. Ocean freight: dry bulk (Handysize/Supramax) and containers, Brazil → US Gulf / Brownsville

### Takeaway
Dry-bulk freight from Brazil to the US Gulf for ~25–40 kt parcels has recently run about US$20–25/t (pig iron to New Orleans, March 2025), and the reverse sulfur route about US$22–24/t (Aug 2025). For Brownsville, plan **US$22–35/t** for a Handysize cargo (smaller port, 40–42 ft draft, small infrequent parcels). Containers (Santos→Houston, Q2 2026: US$2,000–3,000 per 20 ft box) work out to roughly **US$90–150/t** of payload before handling — 4–6× bulk.

### Cited Findings
- Brazil pig-iron (a 25–40 kt Handysize/Supramax trade) freight to New Orleans: about US$20/t from northern Brazil and US$25/t from southern Brazil, described as "stable" (26 Mar 2025); some sellers added US$3–5/t to secure non-Chinese ships when US port fees on Chinese vessels were proposed — [SteelOrbis (26 Mar 2025)](https://www.steelorbis.com/steel-news/latest-news/us-proposal-to-charge-chinese-vessels-poses-big-threat-to-pig-iron-import-market-freight-rate-rise-discussed-1385008.htm)
- Brazil exported 3.3 Mt of pig iron to the US in 2025 (more than half of US pig iron imports of 5.3 Mt), mostly from Rio/Vitória — a well-established Brazil→US Gulf Handysize/Supramax lane — [S&P Global (16 Jul 2026)](https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/metals/071626-us-imposes-new-tariff-on-brazil-but-exempts-pig-iron-rare-earths); cargo concluded at US$445/t FOB Rio/Vitória for October 2026 shipment to the US — [Steel Market Update (4 Sep 2026)](https://www.steelmarketupdate.com/2026/09/04/new-transactions-in-brazil-us-pig-iron-trade/)
- Reverse direction: US Gulf → Brazil sulfur, 35–40 kt parcels, US$22–24/t (7 Aug 2025) — [Argus Sulphur sample report (7 Aug 2025)](https://view.argusmedia.com/rs/584-BUW-606/images/FER-sample-Argus%20Sulphur); US Gulf → Brazil met coal, 70 kt Panamax, US$15.19/t (Apr 2024) — [Hellenic Shipping News (19 Apr 2024)](https://www.hellenicshippingnews.com/?p=1036727)
- Time-charter context: Handysize 40,000 dwt Recalada (Argentina) → US Gulf fixed at US$18,500/day (week to 21 Aug 2026); Baltic Dry Index 2,841 on 21 Aug 2026 — [Baltic Exchange weekly via DCN (21 Aug 2026)](https://www.thedcn.com.au/news/baltic-exchange-weekly-report-21-august-2026). In May 2026, 35,000 dwt transatlantic trips to the US Gulf ~US$9,000/day and to East Coast South America US$6,500–7,000/day — [Banchero Costa via SteelOrbis (May 18–25, 2026)](https://www.steelorbis.com/steel-prices/steel-matters/may-18-25-2026-weekly-market-report-banchero-costa-1455674.htm)
- Atlantic Supramax rates rose ~46% quarter-on-quarter in Q3 2025; Santos→Qingdao Supramax grain TCE up 36% QoQ (no absolute US$/t given) — [S&P Global (2 Oct 2025)](https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/shipping/100225-dry-bulk-quarterly-atlantic-supramax-market-firms-amid-stronger-demand)
- Container rates, Brazil → US, "as of Q2 2026" (ocean freight only, excluding duties, drayage, last mile): Santos→Houston 20 ft US$2,000–3,000, 40 ft US$3,000–4,800; Santos→Miami 20 ft US$1,400–2,200; Santos→Houston/Galveston transit 14–18 days direct, 18–24 with transshipment — [Suaid Global, Importing from Brazil to the USA (pub. 5 Apr 2026, modified 27 Oct 2026)](https://suaidglobal.com/es/insights/import-from-brazil-to-usa/)
- Generic container payload capacities quoted: 20 ft standard ~21.7 t, 40 ft ~26.5 t; origin/destination port, documentation, customs and drayage typically add US$800–2,500 per container (not Brazil-specific) — [Suaid Global, FCL rates 2026](https://suaidglobal.com/pt-br/insights/fcl-container-rates-2026/)
- Santos terminal handling charge (THC): US$164–212 per 20 ft container depending on carrier (ANTAQ study, article Nov 2019) — [Datamar News (25 Nov 2019)](https://datamarnews.com/noticias/study-shows-thc-prices-have-significant-differences-between-major-port-complexes-in-the-country/)
- Sea distance Santos→Houston quoted as 6,968 nm with ~26 days container transit — [Fluent Cargo](https://www.fluentcargo.com/routes/sao-paulo-br/houston-us) (this looks long for a direct route; see Inferences)

### Inferences
- **Bulk (Handysize) estimate for Vitória/Santos → Brownsville:** base US$25/t, range US$20–35/t (2025–26 market). Justification: pig iron Brazil→New Orleans US$20–25/t; Brownsville is ~similar distance but a smaller, less-frequented port, and 5,000 t/month cannot fill a ship more than ~2–4 times a year, so no volume discount and possible part-cargo premiums. A cross-check voyage estimate (my assumptions: ~5,300–5,600 nm direct, ~19 days at sea at 12 knots, ~30 days total including ports/ballast, hire US$15–18.5k/day, ~22 t/day fuel at an assumed ~US$550/t VLSFO, ~US$150k port costs) gives ~US$0.85–1.0 M per voyage, i.e. US$24–29/t for a 35 kt cargo — consistent with the range.
- My rough route estimate (Santos → round the Brazilian bulge → Caribbean → Yucatán Channel → Texas) is ~5,300–5,600 nm, shorter than Fluent Cargo's 6,968 nm figure (which may include container routing); treat distance as uncertain, ±20%.
- **Draft:** Brownsville channel 42 ft and Bulk Cargo Dock 40 ft nominal (see Section 4) → Handysize (~33–34 ft loaded draft) fits fully laden; a Supramax (~42–44 ft) would be draft-restricted/part-laden. Handysize is the realistic vessel class until the 52 ft deepening is complete.
- **Containers (dried powder in FIBCs):** at ~20–24 t net per 20 ft box (US road-weight limits may cap it nearer 18–20 t unless the plant is on-port; heavier boxes need overweight permits — general knowledge, unverified) and US$2,000–3,000 ocean freight, ocean cost ≈ US$85–165/t; add origin THC (~US$8–10/t), destination handling/drayage, FIBC bags and stuffing → all-in roughly US$120–200/t. Brownsville is not a major container port; boxes would most likely route via Houston and truck/rail ~350 miles south (adds roughly US$40–70/t; estimate).
- **Breakbulk FIBCs on a bulk/general-cargo ship:** sits between the two (slower loading, stevedoring per bag); a planning guess of US$45–80/t ocean + handling (no sourced quote found).
- **Transit time** ~15–20 days at sea for bulk; containers 14–24 days.

### Gaps
- No published Baltic index route or broker quote specifically for Brazil → Brownsville; a shipbroker quote (Handysize 30–35 kt, Vitória or Santos → Brownsville) would firm this up.
- No sourced FIBC breakbulk handling rates (US$/bag or US$/t) were found.
- Whether any regular container service calls Brownsville directly was not confirmed.

## 4. Inland transport in Brazil, Brazilian port handling, and Port of Brownsville capabilities and charges

### Takeaway
Trucking ~750–850 km from the Tiros/São Gotardo area to Santos, Vitória or Itaguaí costs about R$170–200/t at the legal minimum freight floor for solid bulk (≈US$30–37/t at an assumed R$5.4/US$), probably more in practice. Brownsville is a genuine dry-bulk port (ores, minerals, steel, aggregates; one Bulk Cargo Dock at 40 ft draft; 42 ft channel being deepened to 52 ft; Class 1 rail connections) with low wharfage (~US$1.37–1.46/t for ores/clays in 2026).

### Cited Findings
**Brazil inland**
- Brazil's legal minimum road freight floor (ANTT "piso mínimo de frete") for **solid bulk ("granel sólido"), Table A (full truckload)**: CCD (cost per km) / CC (fixed load-unload charge) = 6 axles R$7.5641/km + R$680.01; 7 axles R$8.2316/km + R$820.34; 9 axles R$9.4318/km + R$908.91. Updated by ANTT Resolution 6.084/2026 and Portaria SUROC 22/2026, in force since 30 Sep 2026. Floor = distance × CCD + CC (tolls extra) — [Transp.net, ANTT table 2026](https://www.transp.net/blog/posts/tabela-frete-antt-2026-resolucao-6076/)
- Paying below the ANTT floor is subject to fines (electronic enforcement from 2025) — [Transp.net, piso mínimo 2025 fiscalização eletrônica](https://www.transp.net/blog/posts/piso-minimo-frete-2025-fiscalizacao-eletronica/)
- Market road freight indices: Minas Gerais average R$7.07 per km travelled vs national R$6.40 (Oct 2024) — [Diário do Comércio](https://diariodocomercio.com.br/economia/preco-medio-frete-minas-gerais-supera-nacional/); national average R$7.25/km (Sep 2025) — [Tridge/Edenred IFR (21 Oct 2025)](https://www.tridge.com/news/road-freight-drops-to-r-725-per-km-izweur)
- Comparable agricultural lane: Rio Verde (Goiás) → Santos grain freight R$265–351/t in Feb 2026 (peak soy season; ~900+ km) — [IstoÉ Dinheiro / Conab (31 Mar 2026)](https://istoedinheiro.com.br/conab-frete-segue-elevado-com-safra-de-soja-e-pode-subir-mais-em-abril)
- Rail option: VLI runs a fertilizer rail corridor on the FCA (Ferrovia Centro-Atlântica) from the Tiplam terminal (Santos) to a fertilizer terminal in Uberaba (Triângulo Mineiro), operating since 2022 — [Diário do Comércio (4 Nov 2022)](https://diariodocomercio.com.br/economia/vli-inicia-operacoes-em-novo-corredor-de-fertilizantes-para-uberaba/)

**Brownsville**
- Channel design depth 42 ft (deepening to 52 ft under way); turning basin 36 ft; 13 cargo docks of which 6 are liquid docks; dry bulk handled includes "steel bulk materials, ores, scrap, sand, limestone, and grains"; Brownsville & Rio Grande International Railway (~45 miles of on-port track) interchanges with UP, BNSF and CPKC — [Port of Brownsville, Strategic Advantage](https://www.portofbrownsville.com/about/strategic-advantage/)
- Brazos Island Harbor 42→52 ft deepening (17-mile channel) "slated for completion in 2026"; 2023 cargo 11.2 Mt (up 23%), ranked 41st of 150 US ports — [Texas Gulf Coast / TXGulf (19 May 2025)](https://www.txgulf.org/news/89-years-strong-port-of-brownsville-rises-to-no-41-in-us-port-rankings)
- TxDOT profile: 1 bulk cargo dock/grain elevator, 12 general cargo docks, 6 liquid docks; >1 million sq ft covered and >3 million sq ft open storage; main cargoes include "iron ores and minerals" and "aggregates and cement"; 85,216 rail cars FY2023 — [TxDOT Port of Brownsville profile (89th Legislature)](https://www.txdot.gov/content/dam/docs/maritime/port-profiles/port-of-brownsville.pdf)
- Bulk Cargo Dock: 400 ft long, 40 ft nominal vessel draft (39 ft with special fendering), two 25,000 bushel/h gantries and reversible conveyors (grain-oriented); Dock 15 has 4.1 acres of open storage rated 2,500 lb/sq ft; 8 million bbl liquid tank storage being expanded; stevedore Schaefer Stevedoring handles bulk, scrap, steel and containers; 2024 steel imports a record 6 Mt; ~95,000 rail car moves in 2024. ## 6. Radioactive material transport: do thorium/uranium levels trigger IAEA / US DOT Class 7 rules, and what would make shipping harder?

### Takeaway
Natural ores and concentrates processed for purposes other than extracting radionuclides are exempt from radioactive-transport rules if their activity concentration is ≤10× the exempt values — for natural thorium and natural uranium that is 10 Bq/g each, which corresponds roughly to ≤~2,500 ppm thorium or ≤~800 ppm uranium (combined by a sum-of-fractions rule). Above that, the material becomes Class 7 "LSA-I" (UN2912), which can still be shipped (even unpackaged/bulk under conditions) but faces carrier refusals, extra documentation and port restrictions. US nuclear "source material" rules may bite at a lower level (≈0.05 wt% U+Th) — needs verification.

### Cited Findings
- 49 CFR 173.401(b)(4) — the US hazmat radioactive-material rules do not apply to: "Natural material and ores containing naturally occurring radionuclides which are either in their natural state, or which have only been processed for purposes other than for extraction of the radionuclides, and which are not intended to be processed for the use of these radionuclides, provided the activity concentration of the material does not exceed 10 times the exempt material activity concentration values specified in § 173.436, or determined in accordance with the requirements of § 173.433" — [49 CFR 173.401, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/173.401)
- 49 CFR 173.436 exempt values: Th (nat) 1.0 Bq/g and 1.0×10³ Bq consignment; U (nat) 1.0 Bq/g and 1.0×10³ Bq; Th-232 and U-238 10 Bq/g and 1.0×10⁴ Bq; Ra-226 10 Bq/g; "nat" entries include progeny in secular equilibrium — [49 CFR 173.436, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/173.436)
- IAEA SSR-6 para 107(f) mirrors this: the Regulations do not apply to natural materials and ores with naturally occurring radionuclides (possibly processed, but not for radionuclide extraction) if activity concentration ≤10× the Table 2 values — "equates to material containing less than 10 Bq/g of U-nat and 10 Bq/g of Th-nat"; a sum-of-fractions formula is used for mixtures; for most NORM (naturally occurring radioactive material) the concentration, not the total activity, decides. Above the threshold the material is LSA-I, II or III; LSA-I may be transported unpackaged under certain conditions; UN 2912 "Radioactive Material, Low Specific Activity (LSA-I)"; LSA may also go in industrial packages (drums, bags, ISO containers) — [IAEA, Transport of NORM presentation](https://iaea.org/sites/default/files/21/12/7_transport_of_norm.pdf)
- The IMSBC Code (via BIMCO's solid bulk cargo database) carries schedules for "Radioactive material, low specific activity (LSA-I) UN 2912" and "Sand mineral concentrate, radioactive material LSA-I UN 2912" — i.e. bulk sea carriage of LSA-I mineral sands concentrates is provided for — [BIMCO solid cargo database listing](https://bimco.org/Cargo/Solid%20bulk%20cargo/Solid%20bulk%20cargo%20database/Solid_cargo_database%200723/Sand%20mineral%20concentrate%20radioactive%20material%20low%20specific%20activity%20LSAI%20UN%202912) (page could not be fetched; existence from search listing only)
- Example (Feb 2026): ANSTO assessed Lindian's Kangankunde (Malawi) rare-earth concentrate as not radioactive for transport under IAEA SSR-6; the company says avoiding Class 7 preserves carrier flexibility and avoids packaging, documentation, carrier restrictions and oversight obligations; many monazite projects have U/Th high enough to trigger Class 7 — [Engineering News (26 Feb 2026)](https://www.engineeringnews.co.za/article/ansto-ruling-gives-lindian-transport-and-cost-edge-at-kangankunde-2026-02-26)
- TSCA import rules require a *negative* certification for "source, special nuclear, and by-product materials" (they are regulated under nuclear law instead) — [US EPA, TSCA import requirements](https://www.epa.gov/node/77255)

### Inferences
- **Converting to grade (my arithmetic, standard specific activities: Th-232 ≈ 4,060 Bq/g of Th; U-238 ≈ 12,400 Bq/g of U):** 10 Bq/g Th-nat ≈ **2,460 ppm Th** (≈0.28% ThO₂); 10 Bq/g U-nat ≈ **810 ppm U** (≈0.095% U₃O₈). Combined test: (ppm Th ÷ 2,460) + (ppm U ÷ 810) ≤ 1 → exempt from transport rules. Example: 1,000 ppm Th + 300 ppm U → 0.41 + 0.37 = 0.78 → exempt; 1,500 ppm Th + 500 ppm U → 1.23 → Class 7.
- The Tiros fine fraction **concentrates** the REE-bearing minerals (and with them Th/U) relative to ROM ore, so it could exceed the threshold even if ROM ore does not. The REE-bearing fraction is being shipped "for Ti/REE extraction, not radionuclide extraction," so the 10× natural-material exemption applies (it would not if the material were processed to recover U/Th).
- What makes it harder if Class 7: carrier and port "denial of shipment" (many container lines and terminals refuse Class 7), mandatory dose-rate surveys, placarding, UN2912 declarations, radiation-protection programme, possible emergency-response plans, and potential Brazilian export authorisation by CNEN (Brazil's nuclear energy commission) — these are general-knowledge points, not sourced in this pass.
- **Possible lower trigger — US NRC "source material":** from general knowledge, 10 CFR Part 40 defines source material as material containing ≥0.05% by weight U + Th (≈500 ppm combined), with import possibly requiring NRC licensing (10 CFR Part 110 general licences) and affecting TSCA certification and Brownsville plant licensing (residues would be TENORM/source material). This is likely more restrictive than the DOT transport threshold and **must be verified**.

### Gaps
- Th and U assays of the Tiros fine fraction (and of the ROM ore and MREC) are needed — the decisive input.
- NRC source-material threshold and import licensing (10 CFR 40/110) not verified from a primary source in this pass.
- Brazilian CNEN export rules for thorium-bearing concentrates not researched.

## 7. US import requirements for Brazilian mineral concentrates (customs, tariffs as of Nov 2026, TSCA)

### Takeaway
As of late 2026, the US applies a new 25% Section 301 tariff on most Brazilian goods (from 22 Jul 2026) **but titanium ores and concentrates (HTS 2614.00.30/2614.00.60), uranium/thorium ores (2612), rare-earth minerals (2530.90) and compounds (2846), and kaolin (2507) are on the exemption list.** A separate 12.5% "forced-labour" Section 301 tariff on Brazil (from 24 Jul 2026) may also apply unless an exclusion covers the product — this is the key unresolved tariff question. Importers must also file a TSCA certification with US Customs and Border Protection (CBP).

### Cited Findings
- USTR's Section 301 final action on Brazil: 25% additional duty on substantially all Brazilian goods entered on/after 12:01 a.m. ET 22 Jul 2026, reported under HTSUS 9903.05.01; in-transit relief for goods loaded before that time and entered before 29 Jul; exemptions include goods in a specific annex, civil aircraft, some pharmaceuticals, and goods already under Section 232 — [Shapiro (16 Jul 2026)](https://www.shapiro.com/alerts/u-s-announces-25-section-301-tariff-on-most-brazilian-imports/); rare earths and pig iron exempt — [S&P Global (16 Jul 2026)](https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/metals/071626-us-imposes-new-tariff-on-brazil-but-exempts-pig-iron-rare-earths)
- Annex I of the Federal Register notice (heading 9903.05.03 general exclusions) lists 2614.00.30 and 2614.00.60 (titanium ores and concentrates), 2612.10.00 and 2612.20.00 (uranium and thorium ores), 2530.90.10/.20/.80, 2846.10.00/2846.90.20/.40/.80 (rare-earth compounds), 2617.10.00, 2620.30.00/2620.99.50, and 2507.00.00 (kaolin). Sulfur (2503) is not exempt; sulfuric acid (2807) only for pharmaceutical use — [USTR Brazil 301 Final Action FRN (15 Jul 2026)](https://capitaldigital.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Brazil-301-Final-Action-FRN-7-15-2026-final.pdf)
- Exempt categories are justified as "raw materials that if subject to these tariffs could lead to the unavailability of domestic supply," etc. — [USTR FRN (15 Jul 2026)](https://capitaldigital.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Brazil-301-Final-Action-FRN-7-15-2026-final.pdf)
- Brazil's trade-weighted US tariff estimated to rise from 11.0% to 17.5% on 22 Jul 2026; the 10% Section 122 surcharge lapsed 24 Jul 2026 and a separate "forced labour" Section 301 tariff took effect the same day — [Global Trade Alert (16 Jul 2026)](https://globaltradealert.org/blog/brazil-section-301-final-action)
- Forced-labour Section 301 rate for Brazil: 12.5%, effective 24 Jul 2026, stacking with the 25% Brazil tariff (37.5% total for non-excluded goods). Its exclusions (essential raw materials, industrial inputs, Section 232 goods) are separate from the Brazil-301 annex; DHL advises assuming the tariff applies unless the product is specifically excluded — [DHL (2026)](https://www.dhl.com/discover/en-br/regulatory-affairs/us-section-301-tariffs-brazil)
- The US Supreme Court held (20 Feb 2026) that IEEPA does not authorise tariffs, ending the earlier IEEPA-based tariffs on Brazil — [DLA Piper (Feb 2026)](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/02/us-supreme-court-holds-ieepa-does-not-authorize-tariffs) (headline only; not fetched)
- TSCA: every imported chemical substance/mixture needs a positive certification ("all chemical substances in this shipment comply with all applicable rules or orders under TSCA") or negative certification ("not subject to TSCA"), signed by the importer or agent and filed electronically via CBP's ACE system before release; negative certification is required for, among others, source, special nuclear and by-product materials — [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/node/77255); regulation at [19 CFR 12.121](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/19/12.121)

### Inferences
- A Tiros fine concentrate (anatase + REE minerals + kaolinite) would most likely be classified under HTS 2614 (titanium ores and concentrates) → **exempt from the 25% Brazil-301 tariff.** ROM ore would likely also fall in 2614 (or possibly 2530.90). Classification should be confirmed by a customs broker or binding ruling (CBP), since a mixed Ti–REE material could be argued either way.
- The general (MFN) US duty on titanium ores/concentrates is, from general knowledge, "Free" — verify against the HTSUS.
- **Key open risk:** whether HTS 2614 is excluded from the 12.5% forced-labour Section 301 tariff. If not, the duty would be 12.5% of customs value — on a concentrate worth, say, US$200–500/t, that is US$25–60/t, comparable to the whole ocean freight.
- TSCA: unprocessed or only physically beneficiated (crushing, screening, gravity, flotation, drying) ores are, from general knowledge, "naturally occurring substances" automatically on the TSCA Inventory → a routine positive certification. If the concentrate were chemically treated in Brazil (e.g., partly leached or acid-baked), its TSCA status would need checking. If it qualifies as NRC source material (≥0.05% U+Th), a negative certification plus NRC import authority would apply instead (to verify).
- Standard CBP entry: Importer Security Filing ("10+2") for ocean cargo, customs bond, entry/entry summary, Merchandise Processing Fee (0.3464% ad valorem, min/max) and Harbor Maintenance Fee (0.125% of value) — general knowledge, not sourced in this pass.

## 8. Synthesis: indicative end-to-end shipping cost and risk by option (a)–(e)

### Takeaway
Shipping partly dried fines in bulk via Vitória or Santos to Brownsville is the cheapest viable route at roughly **US$75–130 per dry tonne** delivered including drying, versus **US$190–320/t** for dried powder in bulk bags in containers.
