# Keep Tiros acid steps in Brazil

**Run the sulfuric-acid steps at Tiros in Minas Gerais and ship only finished intermediates to Brownsville, Texas.** Doing the acid bake in Texas would mean shipping 1.1–2.7 tonnes of fine, clay-rich rock across the ocean for every tonne of acid used. Running it in Brazil means shipping in only about 0.31 t of sulfur per tonne of acid, or about 1.15 t once you count the caustic soda and products that also have to cross the ocean. Texas does have cheaper reagents. Gulf Coast sulfur, caustic soda (sodium hydroxide, NaOH) and hydrochloric acid (HCl) are all cheaper there. By our screening estimate that saves about US$20–90 per tonne of ore. Shipping the rock and getting rid of its residue in Texas costs about US$50–140 per tonne of ore. The cargo also has two built-in problems. Fines this small are likely to be classed as a cargo that can liquefy at sea. And no one has yet measured the thorium and uranium in the fines. A Texas acid plant would also leave behind the acid-bake residue (about 0.31 t per tonne of ore), a sodium sulfate brine (about 0.37 t of dissolved salt per tonne of ore, from the large caustic dose), and whatever thorium and radium end up in them. Texas permits these wastes through several agencies. The closest precedent, Lynas's rare-earth plant at Seadrift, Texas, was cancelled in August 2026 after its wastewater permit stalled. One more problem sits underneath the whole comparison: the July 2026 preliminary economic assessment (PEA, an early-stage cost study) prices both acid and caustic soda below 2026 market levels. It also contains inconsistencies large enough to change any site comparison. Fix those first.

*How to read this report.* "Ore" or "run-of-mine (ROM) ore" means rock as mined. The baseline is the Tiros NI 43-101 PEA (NI 43-101 is the Canadian standard for public mining reports), effective 10 July 2026, written by Norda Stelo. PEA page references (for example "PEA p.276") point to that report. Unless stated otherwise, quantities are the PEA's: 500,000 t/yr of ore, 0.374 t of 96% sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) per tonne of ore, and 0.205 t of NaOH per tonne of ore. Figures marked "derived" are our own arithmetic. Figures marked "estimate" are planning assumptions, not quotes. The student brief proposes 5,000 t/month of ore (60,000 t/yr) for Brownsville. That is 12% of the PEA scale, and we scale the numbers to it where it matters.

## The rock outweighs the acid nearly three to one

Most of the acid goes into rock that is not the product. In the fines circuit, the material below 75 micrometres (µm, thousandths of a millimetre) is first roasted at 600 °C with carbon monoxide. It then passes through magnetic separators that pull out iron minerals (LIMS and WHIMS, low- and wet high-intensity magnetic separation). After that it is "baked" with 96% sulfuric acid at 300 °C. The PEA assumes an acid-regeneration step at about 600–680 °C and calls it "conceptual and untested." Then comes a water leach at 80 °C. Titanium is precipitated from the leach liquor with NaOH. Iron and aluminium are removed with magnesium hydroxide. Finally, a mixed rare-earth carbonate (MREC) is precipitated with soda ash (PEA p.266, 272–273). In testwork, acid consumption was 1.111 kg per kg of bake feed. The rare-earth oxides made up only 0.663% of that feed. So about 99% of the acid reacts with iron, aluminium, titanium, phosphorus and magnesium (PEA p.214, derived).

Which material you ship sets how many tonnes cross the ocean. In the pilot-feed proxy test (sample BK19), natural plus ground fines were 71.1% of ore mass. The roasted, magnetically cleaned bake feed was 58.1%. The leach residue was 30.8% (PEA Table 13.17, p.217–218). A second test series implies the bake feed could be as low as about 42% of ore (PEA p.214).

| Material crossing the ocean | Tonnes per tonne of ore | Tonnes per tonne of 96% acid used |
|---|---|---|
| Raw ore, dry basis (moisture not stated in PEA) | 1.00 | **2.67** |
| All fines below 75 µm, as filter cake at ~25% moisture (estimate) | 0.95 | **2.54** |
| Same fines, dried to ~10% to get below the safe shipping moisture | 0.79 | **2.11** |
| Same fines, bone dry | 0.71 | 1.90 |
| Dry calcine = roasted, magnetically cleaned acid-bake feed | 0.42–0.58 | **1.12–1.55** |
| 96% sulfuric acid itself | 0.374 | **1.00** |
| Sulfur to make that acid in an on-site acid plant | 0.12 | **0.31** |
| Brazil case, everything that crosses: sulfur + NaOH + fine TiO₂ product + MREC | 0.43 | **1.15** |

The "5× more tonnage" figure that comes from comparing rock with sulfur alone overstates the gap. Brazil already imports most of its caustic soda from the US Gulf. Brazil took 50–60% of US caustic exports in 2024–early 2025 ([S&P Global](https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/chemicals/032825-brazils-pulp-paper-industry-to-increase-demand-for-us-caustic-soda-plstico-brasil)). And the fine titanium dioxide (TiO₂) product and the MREC must be shipped out of Brazil eventually. Once those count against the Brazil case, shipping dry calcine moves about **1.0–1.35×** the ocean tonnage, and shipping wet fines about **2.2×**. Calcine reaches break-even only at the low 42% bake-feed yield. Shipping raw ore makes things worse in another way. The PEA expects tailings of about 80% of ore mass (PEA p.344). At 5,000 t/month, that means about 48,000 t/yr of waste rock and residue would be shipped to Texas only to be landfilled there.

## Texas sulfur is cheaper, but Brazil can buy it

Sulfur is the main cost of making sulfuric acid, usually 70–80% of it ([Alliance Chemical](https://alliancechemical.com/blogs/news/sulfuric-acid-plant-curtailments-2026-catalyst-restart-cost)). One tonne of sulfur burned in a contact acid plant makes about 3.06 t of acid. The plant also produces about 1.4 t of high-pressure steam and about 260 kWh of net power per tonne of acid ([JSAIMM 2009](https://scielo.org.za/pdf/jsaimm/v109n8/05.pdf)). In 2026 the sulfur price became the dominant risk. Brazil's delivered sulfur price (CFR, "cost and freight", meaning the price at the Brazilian port) was about **US$182/t in early 2025**. It reached **US$1,200/t in June–early July 2026** ([Sulphur 426, CRU](https://www.bcinsight.crugroup.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2026/09/Sulphur_426.pdf)). Several shocks pushed it up:

- Russia restricted sulfur exports from 1 November 2025.
- The Strait of Hormuz closed to commercial dry bulk ships from about 28 February 2026 ([Policy Center for the New South](https://www.policycenter.ma/publications/sulfur-market-caught-vice-strait-hormuz-crisis)).
- China halted sulfuric acid exports from May 2026 ([CRU](https://www.bcinsight.crugroup.com/2026/05/27/chinas-sulphuric-acid-export-halt-tightens-global-supply/)).
- Kazakhstan suspended sulfur exports on 27 June 2026.
- A Ukrainian strike on 24 August 2026 hit Astrakhan, which makes more than 60% of Russia's sulfur.

By 29 October 2026 Brazil spot sulfur had eased to **US$860–880/t**, but the fourth-quarter contract was still about US$1,036. Freight from the Middle East to Brazil was about US$165/t, against US$28–29 a year earlier ([ChemPortal](https://www.chemportal.com.tr/markets/sulphur.html)). Acid followed sulfur. Brazil acid CFR went from US$125–130/t in September 2025 to about **US$350–370/t** in August–October 2026 ([Argus Sulphuric Acid, Sept 2025](https://view.argusmedia.com/rs/584-BUW-606/images/APAC-RSMT-FER-2025-10-01-SAM-FER-Fertilizer%20Campaign-Argus%20Sulphuric%20Acid.pdf?version=0); [Sulphur 426](https://www.bcinsight.crugroup.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/7/2026/09/Sulphur_426.pdf)). The commodity analyst CRU forecasts Chilean acid at about US$215/t by late 2027 ([Reuters via MINING.COM](https://www.mining.com/web/sulphuric-acid-prices-due-to-extend-decline-in-2027-cru-says)).

Texas has a structural sulfur edge. Texas and Louisiana produce about 54% of US sulfur, mostly recovered from refineries ([USGS 2026](https://pubs.usgs.gov/periodicals/mcs2026/mcs2026-sulfur.pdf)). The Tampa liquid-sulfur contract for Q4 2026 was about US$615 per long ton, against Brazil's roughly US$1,036 contract. Our screening model puts acid made from sulfur at **about US$230–350/t at Brownsville** and **US$320–425/t at Tiros** at late-2026 prices. That is an advantage of roughly **US$25–150 per tonne of acid**, depending on whether you compare spot or contract prices. Two facts shrink that edge in practice.

First, the US Gulf is already Brazil's cheapest sulfur source. Bulk sulfur from the US Gulf to Brazil cost **US$26–33/t** in freight through 2025–2026, and that rate stayed stable while Middle East routes spiked ([Argus Sulphur, Sept 2025](https://www.ggttrade.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Argus-Sulphur-2025-09-04.pdf); [ChemPortal](https://www.chemportal.com.tr/markets/sulphur.html)). Since sulfur is only 0.327 t per tonne of acid, a Tiros plant can buy Gulf sulfur for about US$10 of ocean freight per tonne of acid. Trucking it the 783 km from the Port of Santos to Tiros ([Rome2rio](https://www.rome2rio.com/s/Santos/Tiros)) adds about US$13–23 per tonne of acid (estimate). Hauling liquid acid inland instead would cost US$50–90 per tonne. That is the structural reason to haul sulfur and make acid at the mine.

Second, scale. At the PEA's 180,000 t/yr of acid (100% basis), a sulfur-burning plant pays for itself. Chile's Marimaca priced a new 150,000 t/yr plant at US$50–60 M installed ([CRU/BC Insight](https://www.bcinsight.crugroup.com/2025/09/19/marimaca-to-acquire-acid-plant/)). Scaling that up gives about US$56–67 M, or about US$37–44 of capital charge per tonne of acid. The plant would also produce about 5.9 MW of net power, nearly double the PEA site's demand of about 3 MW (derived). At the Brownsville brief's 5,000 t/month of ore, acid demand is only about 22,400 t/yr (about 65 t/day). A plant that small would carry a capital charge of roughly US$85–100 per tonne of acid (derived). A small Texas plant would therefore buy merchant acid at about **US$380–420/t delivered**, which is no cheaper than making acid at Tiros. **At pilot scale, Texas's sulfur advantage largely disappears.**

Volatility matters more than the difference between sites. Each US$100/t change in sulfur price moves acid cost by about US$33/t, or about US$6 M/yr at PEA scale. The 2024–2026 range of sulfur prices alone represents about US$70 M/yr. That is roughly 64% of the PEA's gross operating cost of US$109.5 M/yr (derived). In Brazil, an on-site sulfur burner with a US Gulf supply contract buys power, steam for acid regeneration and supply security. It works as a hedge, not as a bet on any one site.

## Caustic soda is the bigger bill and modestly favors Texas

NaOH is the PEA's largest single reagent cost: 102,534 t/yr at US$420/t, or US$43.1 M/yr and US$86.1 per tonne of ore (PEA Table 21.8, p.341). The PEA price looks low. US Gulf export prices (FOB, "free on board", meaning the price at the loading port before freight) for liquid caustic ran **US$330–490 per dry metric tonne** (dmt, priced per tonne of 100% NaOH) between December 2025 and May 2026 ([S&P Global](https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/chemicals/021626-europe-turns-to-asia-for-caustic-soda-amid-challenging-us-arb); [Argus, 22 May 2026](https://view.argusmedia.com/rs/584-BUW-606/images/CHE-Sample-report-Chlor-Alkali-20260522.pdf)). US domestic contract prices were higher still, at US$655–690 per dry short ton. Brazil adds costs on top: ocean freight, a 7.2% import tariff on liquid caustic ([Tabelas Fiscais](https://tabelasfiscais.com.br/ncm/28151200)), and roughly 800 km of trucking that is paid on wet tonnes. Liquid caustic in Brazil (cost, insurance and freight) was about US$513 per dry tonne in Q1 2026 ([Chemtradeasia](https://www.chemtradeasia.com/market-insights/caustic-soda-flakes-price-index-2026-supply)). Supply is getting tighter: Braskem's Maceió plant, about 25% of Brazil's chlor-alkali capacity, has been idled ([Argus, Nov 2025](https://view.argusmedia.com/rs/584-BUW-606/images/CHE-Argus-Chlor-Alkali-Outlook-Report-Sample-Nov2025.pdf?version=0)). Brazil closed its anti-dumping case on US liquid caustic in 2022 without imposing a duty ([Circular SECEX 34/2022](https://www.normasbrasil.com.br/norma/circular-34-2022_434958.html)). Delivered to Tiros, we estimate **US$550–700/t**. That would add about US$13–29 M/yr to the PEA's total. Delivered to Brownsville we estimate **US$500–650 per dry tonne** for 50% liquid, which also avoids the 40–60% premium for solid flake. The Texas saving is therefore about **US$50–150 per tonne of NaOH**, or US$10–31 per tonne of ore.

Hydrochloric acid matters less. It is used only in the coarse circuit, which would stay in Brazil anyway. US prices were about US$180–350/t through 2025–Q1 2026, and Brazilian prices delivered to Cubatão were about US$240–430/t ([ChemAnalyst](https://www.chemanalyst.com/Pricing-data/hydrochloric-acid-61)). The quarter-to-quarter figures do not reconcile, so treat them as ±30%. The PEA's US$200/t is at the low end for an inland Minas Gerais site. The total gap between countries is only about US$4–8 M/yr even at full scale. Magnesium hydroxide (about US$800/t in the US against about US$1,030/t in Brazil) and soda ash (about US$183/t in Wyoming) also favor Texas. Their volumes are small ([IMARC](https://www.imarcgroup.com/news/magnesium-hydroxide-price-index); [ChemAnalyst](https://chemanalyst.com/Pricing-data/soda-ash-76)).

Combining both sections gives a rough comparison per tonne of ore:

| Screening estimate, US$ per tonne of ore | Low | High |
|---|---|---|
| Texas gain: cheaper acid (near zero at pilot scale) | 9 | 56 |
| Texas gain: cheaper NaOH | 10 | 31 |
| **Total Texas reagent gain** | **19** | **87** |
| Texas cost: drying and shipping calcine or fines from Tiros to Brownsville | 41 | 107 |
| Texas cost: residue disposal in Texas instead of the planned Tiros dry stack | 12 | 31 |
| Texas cost: sodium sulfate brine treatment, radioactive licensing, running two sites | not priced | not priced |
| **Total Texas logistics and waste cost** | **53** | **138+** |

At mid-range values, Brazil comes out ahead by roughly US$40 per tonne of ore. That is about US$20 M/yr at PEA scale. Texas wins only if every Texas-favorable assumption lands at the same time. Treat these numbers as a screening model for testing assumptions, not a cost estimate.

## Wet clay fines make a hard, liquefiable cargo

The International Maritime Solid Bulk Cargoes Code (IMSBC Code) sorts dry bulk cargoes into groups. Group A covers cargoes that can liquefy if shipped too wet: the cargo turns into a slurry, shifts in the hold, and can capsize the ship. The code requires any damp, fine-grained cargo to be flow-tested ([Gard](https://gard.no/en/insights/update-on-group-bauxite-that-can-cause-vessel-instability)). The Tiros fines are 100% below 75 µm and contain kaolinite clay. That is far finer than the 1 mm and 2.5 mm size cut-offs that put bauxite into Group A ([IMO CCC.1/Circ.2/Rev.1](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/MediaCentre/PressBriefings/Documents/CCC.1-Circ.2-Rev.1%20-%20Carriage%20Of%20Bauxite%20Which%20May%20Liquefy.pdf)). The code's generic "Clay" entry is not Group A. Even so, a cargo of Malaysian kaolinite ball clay liquefied and listed 15° at sea, and a sister ship was lost with significant loss of life ([West P&I](https://www.westpandi.com/globalassets/loss-prevention/loss-prevention-safety-alerts/131125-sa018---cargo-liquefaction-incident---ball-clay-from-malaysia.pdf)). Between 2013 and 2022, liquefaction caused 5 of 26 bulk-carrier losses and 70 of 104 deaths ([gCaptain](https://gcaptain.com/intercargo-warns-against-complacency-as-liquefaction-risk-looms-over-dry-bulk-shipping)). **Plan on Group A.** To ship in bulk, the cargo must be below its Transportable Moisture Limit (TML, normally 90% of the moisture at which a lab sample starts to flow). The shipper needs a TML certificate less than six months old and a moisture certificate less than seven days old ([Swedish Club](https://www.swedishclub.com/uploads/2025/12/Cargo-Advice-Liquefaction.pdf)).

Meeting that limit means pressure filtration followed by thermal drying. Vacuum filters stop working well below a mean particle size of about 25–30 µm, and plate-shaped clay particles hold water and clog filter cloths ([Metso](https://www.metso.com/insights/blog/mining-and-metals/beginners-guide-to-concentrate-filtration/)). Drying takes about 3.2–4.5 GJ per tonne of water evaporated ([911Metallurgist](https://www.911metallurgist.com/btu-energy-required-dry-ores-concentrates)). Taking a 25%-moisture cake down to 10% therefore needs about 0.8 GJ per dry tonne of solids (derived). Packing the material in bulk bags (flexible intermediate bulk containers, FIBCs) takes it outside the bulk-cargo rules. It does not stop wet bags from slumping and shifting, though ([Gard](https://gard.no/insights/bulk-cargo-in-jumbo-bags-understanding-the-risks/)). The dry calcine is the best option because it leaves the kiln dry. But it is a dusty sub-75 µm powder. Whether it caks, picks up moisture or re-oxidizes in transit has not been tested.

Freight is affordable at the low end and expensive at the high end:

- **Bulk.** Brazilian pig iron to New Orleans in 25–40 kt cargoes ran US$20–25/t ([SteelOrbis](https://www.steelorbis.com/steel-news/latest-news/us-proposal-to-charge-chinese-vessels-poses-big-threat-to-pig-iron-import-market-freight-rate-rise-discussed-1385008.htm)). Brownsville's channel is 42 ft deep and its Bulk Cargo Dock is 40 ft. Wharfage is only US$1.46/t for ores and concentrates ([Port of Brownsville Tariff No. 7](https://www.portofbrownsville.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/2025-09-17-Tariff-No-7.pdf)). We estimate US$22–35/t ocean freight on a Handysize ship (about 25–40 kt).
- **Containers.** Santos to Houston cost US$2,000–3,000 per 20 ft container in Q2 2026 ([Suaid Global](https://suaidglobal.com/es/insights/import-from-brazil-to-usa/)). That is roughly US$90–165 per tonne, and Brownsville is not a major container port.
- **Trucking in Brazil.** Brazil's legal minimum freight rate for solid bulk on 9-axle trucks is R$9.43/km plus R$909 per load ([Transp.net](https://www.transp.net/blog/posts/tabela-frete-antt-2026-resolucao-6076/)). That puts the haul from Tiros to the port at about US$30–45/t.

All in, partly dried fines shipped in bulk cost about **US$85–150 per dry tonne** delivered. Bagged powder in containers costs about US$200–360 per tonne. At 5,000 t/month, one Handysize cargo holds 6–8 months of production. That means 30–40 kt of covered storage at each port.

Tariffs are mostly neutral. The 25% Section 301 tariff on Brazilian goods that took effect on 22 July 2026 excludes titanium ores and concentrates (tariff codes HTS 2614), uranium and thorium ores, rare-earth compounds (HTS 2846) and kaolin ([USTR notice](https://capitaldigital.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Brazil-301-Final-Action-FRN-7-15-2026-final.pdf)). A separate 12.5% "forced-labour" Section 301 tariff took effect on 24 July 2026. It has its own exclusions, and we could not confirm whether it covers these products ([DHL](https://www.dhl.com/discover/en-br/regulatory-affairs/us-section-301-tariffs-brazil)). That question affects concentrate under both options and the much higher-value MREC under both options, so it does not favor either site. US importers must also file a certification under the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA) with US Customs ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/node/77255)).

Radioactivity in transport depends on the concentration of radioactive elements. Under both the US Department of Transportation (DOT) rules and the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) rules, natural ores are exempt from radioactive-material transport rules up to 10 times the exempt activity values. That works out to 10 Bq/g each for natural thorium and natural uranium (a becquerel, Bq, is one radioactive decay per second) ([49 CFR 173.401](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/49/173.401); [IAEA](https://iaea.org/sites/default/files/21/12/7_transport_of_norm.pdf)). In grade terms, that is about 2,460 ppm thorium or 810 ppm uranium, combined using a sum-of-fractions rule (derived). Above that, the cargo becomes Class 7 radioactive material (low specific activity, UN 2912). Many carriers and ports turn Class 7 cargo away. The lower limit that matters more is the 0.05% (500 ppm) uranium-plus-thorium "source material" threshold for licensing in Texas ([TCEQ](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/permitting/radmat/uranium/norm.html)). The fines and the bake feed are only 1.2–1.3× richer in rare earths than the ore (1.11% and 1.25% against 0.94% total rare-earth oxide, PEA Table 13.17), so they may stay below that threshold. **The PEA contains no thorium or uranium assay for any stream** (PEA p.376).

## Texas would inherit the residue, the thorium question and the brine

If the acid steps run in Brownsville, these wastes stay in Texas:

- **Acid-bake leach residue.** About 0.31 t per tonne of ore, mostly silica (PEA p.272). This is the stream most likely to carry radium and possibly thorium (inference).
- **Magnesium hydroxide filter cake** with iron, aluminium, potassium and calcium. The PEA does not give its tonnage.
- **Titanium hydrolysis reject** (any off-spec titanium precipitate). Not quantified.
- **A dissolved sodium sulfate stream.** The PEA chose magnesium hydroxide "to avoid gypsum formation" (PEA p.371), and it uses only 2,397 t/yr. Almost no gypsum forms. But NaOH added to sulfate liquor ends up as dissolved sodium sulfate. By our stoichiometry, 102,534 t/yr of caustic plus the soda ash makes about **183,000 t/yr of sodium sulfate (about 0.37 t per tonne of ore, or about 22,000 t/yr at the 5,000 t/month Brownsville scale)**. Neutralizing the water does not take it out of solution. It needs evaporation and crystallization, deep-well injection, or a discharge permit. The PEA notes only that "final water discharge quality needs definition" (PEA p.363).

Under federal hazardous-waste law (the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, RCRA), none of these streams qualifies for the Bevill exclusion. That exemption covers only 20 named mineral-processing wastes, and neither sulfate-process TiO₂ wastes nor rare-earth wastes are on the list ([40 CFR 261.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-I/part-261/subpart-A/section-261.4)). Each stream must be tested for corrosivity and leachable metals, then classed as hazardous waste or as Texas Class 1, 2 or 3 industrial waste. Class 1 is the most restrictive non-hazardous class. It applies to any solid with a pH of 2 or below, among other triggers ([30 TAC §335.505](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/30-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-335-505)). Leach tests on the Tiros tailings already exceed Brazilian limits for arsenic, cadmium, chromium, nickel, lead and other metals (PEA p.304).

Radioactivity in these wastes is called naturally occurring radioactive material (NORM). Texas divides its regulation by agency:

- **The Department of State Health Services (DSHS)** regulates NORM under a general license. Material is exempt at or below 30 pCi/g of radium-226/228 (a picocurie per gram is 0.037 Bq/g), or 150 pCi/g of other radionuclides ([25 TAC §289.259](https://law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/25-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-289-259)).
- **The Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ)** has "sole and exclusive" authority over processing source material, meaning material with 0.05% or more uranium plus thorium ([Tex. Health & Safety Code §401.2625](https://law.counselstack.com/statute/tx/HS/401.2625)). TCEQ's rules allow that "unrefined, unprocessed ore may be exempt above 0.05%" ([TCEQ RG-486](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/downloads/permitting/radioactive-materials/publications/rg-486-radioactive-material-exemptions.pdf)). Roasted calcine has been processed, so it cannot claim that exemption.

TCEQ's uncontested permit averages are 241 days for a new air permit, 155 days for a minor wastewater permit and 509 days for an industrial and hazardous waste permit. A uranium-program licence renewal averaged 862 days, and too few new radioactive-material licences have been processed to give an average ([TCEQ Biennial Report](https://www.tceq.texas.gov/downloads/publications/sfr/srf-057-24/biennial-report-23-24-appendix-b_x.pdf)). Texas has only one commercial radioactive-waste disposal site, Waste Control Specialists (WCS) in Andrews County, more than 600 miles from Brownsville, and it publishes no prices ([WCS](https://www.wcstexas.com/about/our-facilities/)).

Brownsville adds risks of its own. Environmental-justice groups and the City of Port Isabel got the federal approvals for two LNG (liquefied natural gas) terminals overturned in 2024 ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/liquefied-natural-gas/a-us-court-struck-down-permits-for-controversial-lng-projects-in-texas)). The same coalition is opposing the proposed port refinery ([Border Chronicle](https://www.theborderchronicle.com/trump-announces-first-new-u-s-refinery-in-50-years-at-port-of-brownsville-reviving-local-resistance/)). In 2024 the region's reservoirs fell to 22%, and Texas's last sugar mill closed for lack of water ([Texas Tribune](https://www.texastribune.org/2024/04/18/south-texas-drought-agriculture/)). The closest precedent is Lynas's rare-earth plant at Seadrift, Texas, which had up to US$258 M of Department of Defense backing. It stalled over "unresolved wastewater disposal permit issues" ([SMM](https://news.metal.com/newscontent/103646309-lynas-texas-rare-earth-plant-plan-stalled-over-wastewater-permit-issues)), and Lynas confirmed in August 2026 that it "will no longer proceed" ([Lynas FY26 Annual Report](https://announcements.asx.com.au/asxpdf/20260826/pdf/0737nx07s3tnf7.pdf)). Lynas had also planned to import MREC that was already cracked, which would have left the radioactive residues in Australia. Tiros acid steps in Texas would generate them in Texas.

In Brazil, the residues go to the lined, filtered dry-stack facility the PEA already designs (PEA p.305). Brazil's nuclear regulator (CNEN, now ANSN) regulates mining-industrial facilities under standard NN 4.01. Facilities between 10 and 500 Bq/g need a simplified safety report ([CNEN NN 4.01](https://repositorio.mctic.gov.br/bitstream/mctic/5130/1/2002_nn_401_requisitos_de_seguranca_e_protecao_radiologica_para_instalacoes_minero-industriais.pdf)). Minas Gerais's post-Brumadinho law ("Never Again a Sea of Mud") effectively rules out new tailings dams near communities and pushes projects toward dry stacking ([Earthworks](https://earthworks.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/Global-Benchmarking-of-Mining-Waste-Rules-–-Brazil.pdf)). Brazilian licensing is also slow: we assume 24–48 months, and the new General Environmental Licensing Law (Law 15,190/2025) is being challenged in court ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/pt/insights/publications/2025/12/brazilian-congress-overrides-presidential-vetoes-to-the-general-environmental-licensing-law-and-approves-new-rules-on-the-special-environmental-license)). But that licensing is already on the project's critical path. A Texas acid plant does not remove it. It adds a second, parallel set of permits.

## Seven PEA numbers to fix before choosing a site

The site decision depends on reagent quantities and prices that the PEA does not support well.

1. **Rare-earth recovery.** The process section (Section 17) gives 67.0% recovery, or 3,636 t/yr of total rare-earth oxide (TREO, all rare-earth elements expressed as oxides). The economic model uses 4,367 t/yr, which is 80.5% of the 5,426 t/yr in the feed. The difference is about US$29 M/yr of revenue (PEA p.275, p.349, derived).
2. **Acid regeneration.** The PEA calls it "conceptual and untested" and states no recycle percentage (PEA p.272). Raw testwork consumption was about 459 kg/t of ore, while the design figure is 359 kg/t (100% basis). That implies a credit of about 22%. In testwork only about 30% of the acid dose stayed unreacted (PEA p.214–215). Recovering acid locked up in iron and aluminium sulfates would need roasting at 800–1,000 °C, which is how sulfate-route TiO₂ plants do it ([Umweltbundesamt](https://www.umweltbundesamt.de/sites/default/files/medien/publikation/long/3226.pdf)).
3. **Caustic soda.** The PEA does not explain why so much is needed (205 kg per tonne of ore). By our arithmetic, neutralizing the roughly 0.20 t of unreacted acid per tonne of ore seen in testwork would need about 80,000 t/yr of NaOH, roughly 80% of the PEA's tonnage. That is the same free acid the regeneration step is supposed to recover. If regeneration works, caustic demand should fall. If caustic neutralizes the free acid, there is less acid to regenerate. The PEA cannot count both.
4. **Gypsum and brine.** Magnesium hydroxide avoids gypsum, but the caustic creates about 183,000 t/yr of dissolved sodium sulfate. The PEA has no effluent volume or treatment cost for it, and Tiros's annual evaporation (1,450 mm) is roughly equal to its rainfall (1,415 mm) (PEA p.305), so evaporation ponds would remove little water. Sodium also risks rare-earth losses as sodium–rare-earth double sulfates (PEA p.273).
5. **Old reagent prices.** At 2026 delivered prices, acid (US$400–460/t instead of US$170) and NaOH (US$550–700/t instead of US$420) add about **US$56–83 M/yr** to a gross operating cost of US$109.5 M/yr (derived).
6. **Product tonnes.** The 90,255 t/yr of "TiO₂ product" equals the recovered contained TiO₂, but it is priced as concentrate. The MREC grade appears as 52%, an implied 70.3%, and 95–96.4% in different places (PEA p.314, p.349).
7. **Radioactivity.** No thorium or uranium assays or deportment data (which streams these elements end up in) exist for any stream. Yet those numbers decide the transport class, the Texas licensing route and how the residue is disposed of in both countries.

## Conclusion

The comparison changes once caustic soda is counted. Comparing sulfur with rock alone makes Brazil look five times better on tonnage. Caustic and products narrow that to 1.0–2.2×. The sodium sulfate brine from the caustic, rather than the acid itself, becomes the waste most likely to sink a Texas permit, as it did at Seadrift. So a Brazil-first decision is only half the answer. The highest-value engineering work in either country is cutting the caustic: replacing NaOH, recycling the free acid, or crystallizing and selling the sulfate. That work saves more than the site choice does. And at the brief's 5,000 t/month, the Texas reagent advantage shrinks to mostly caustic, because a 65 t/day acid plant is too small to pay off.

Brownsville should start as the downstream end of the chain. It would receive dry TiO₂ concentrate and low-radioactivity MREC, and later add rare-earth separation and titanium tetrachloride (TiCl₄) or titanium sponge modules. The PEA itself warns that the current TiO₂ products need further upgrading before chlorination (PEA p.315). Revisit Texas acid processing only if testing shows all of the following: uranium plus thorium below 500 ppm in the calcine and residues; a dry calcine that ships cleanly under the IMSBC Code; a neutralization route that does not produce a large sulfate brine; and a sulfur shock that hits Brazil but not the US Gulf. The tests that decide this are cheap compared with either plant. In priority order: thorium and uranium deportment across every stream; TML and flow-moisture tests on fines and calcine; pressure-filter cake moisture; leach testing and Texas waste classification of the residues; and a locked-cycle test of acid regeneration with a measured caustic balance.

## Update, model v0.5: the Brownsville rare-earth module

Research note 6 (`docs/research/06-rare-earth-module.md`) tests Steve's idea of separating Tiros's carbonate into light and heavy rare earths in Brownsville. Three findings:
- **Tiros is a light rare-earth deposit.** NdPr is about three-quarters of the value; dysprosium and terbium are under 0.4% of the oxide.
- **Separating Tiros's carbonate alone does not pay.** It adds only about US$7–13/kg, close to the cost of separation, at any size or price case.
- **A hub can pay.** A Brownsville plant that also separates other Brazilian producers' carbonate for a fee, with government grants, can be worth around US$100M. It depends on securing several thousand t/yr of other producers' feed at fees near the top of what they would accept.
